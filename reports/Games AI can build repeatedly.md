# Build puzzles a solver can prove

An AI coding agent can build one family of games repeatedly and to a dependable standard: **turn-based logic puzzles where the whole board is visible and the result depends only on which moves the player makes, never on timing or a steady hand.** The family covers jam-and-escape puzzles (car-out parking, arrow tap-away, sliding blocks), sort puzzles (water, balls, nuts on bolts), draw-a-path grids, tile-match and classic logic grids. In each, a small program called a **solver** can try every possible move. In under a second it proves a level can be won and gives it an exact difficulty score, such as the fewest moves needed. That turns content, the agent's weakest skill in every 2026 benchmark, into its strongest, and it lets the owner check the agent's claims instead of trusting them. These genres also sell: sort, screw and block puzzles drove mobile puzzle growth in 2025, queue-sort games are estimated to bring **52%** of players back the next day against a typical 22%, and two parking-jam games hold **about 21–23 million plays** each on YouTube Playables. The games at the top of Playables' all-time chart are the ones an agent cannot repeat. 3D runners need studio art and reflex tuning that no solver measures, and physics drawing puzzles cannot be checked exactly. This project's own physics puzzle shipped **two unplayable builds while every test passed**, kept about **four in ten** level ideas, and never generated a level that looked new. No portal bans AI-built games; what they punish is near-duplicates. The business that repeats is therefore one shared engine with a **different player decision** in each game, hundreds of solver-proven levels and a daily puzzle inside each game, and real-player testing on Playgama's agent-friendly Sandbox before the bigger portals. Income expectations should stay modest, because an audience, not build speed, decides what a small web game earns. Most sources could be read only as search-engine summaries, and vendor and self-reported figures are marked as such.

## Agents ship working code and stumble on content, art and self-checking

The 2026 benchmarks that ask agents to build whole games all draw the line in the same place. On **GameCraft-Bench**, 140 tasks in the Godot engine across 15 kinds of game, agents' games built and launched **93–100% of the time**. Yet the best agent scored only **about 41%** against a hidden marking scheme, and its two weakest areas were **content depth (39.5%)** and **art and presentation (36.9%)**. The authors write that agents "often implement recognizable mechanics" but "struggle to deliver complete games with sufficient content, functional visual feedback, and coherent presentation" ([GameCraft-Bench](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2606.17861)). **GameDevBench** found the same slope inside one engine: success fell from **51.4% on gameplay tasks to 33.0% on 2D-graphics tasks**, and simply letting one model see its own output lifted it from **41.1% to 52.0%** ([GameDevBench](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2602.11103)). **GameXpert-Bench** uses browser games like the ones this owner ships. It concludes that agents are better at "producing playable foundations and implementing explicit requirements" than at "discovering defects, verifying runtime behavior, or preserving functionality through changes" ([GameXpert-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.21833)). In plain terms, an agent will reliably hand over a game that runs. It will not reliably hand over one with enough good levels, a pleasing look and no new breakages, unless something outside the agent does the checking.

Developers who actually finished games split the work the same way: the agent writes code, a bot or simulation turns the game into numbers, and a human plays and judges how it feels. In *Reptile Catcher*, a one-day build, Claude changed a number and then ran a bot that measured how long play lasted. The bot was later split into five "player personalities". The developer also changed which animals appear in the game to suit what the image tool could draw ([dev.to](https://dev.to/krenin88/building-a-game-demo-with-claude-code-in-one-day-215g)). On a one-day platformer, Claude drove a hidden browser that jumped the gaps, which caught more than a clean compile would. When a tester said one character "felt more fluid", Claude traced the difference to the animation frames, because the physics settings were identical ([dev.to](https://dev.to/tiagovilasboas/i-built-a-full-pwa-game-in-one-day-with-claude-opus-55-and-claude-code-here-is-the-real-token-41p7)). A non-coder's Telegram RPG used "tens of thousands of simulated fights" for balance while the author kept the design calls ([RetroStyle Games](https://retrostylegames.com/blog/claude-code-game-dev-case-study/)). A framework of 49 agents built four games from one brief at different levels of process, and the build that wrote **30 planning documents ranked last**: "the extra 29 documents bought traceability, not a better game." The same framework sets the rule that "a passing test says nothing about a menu drawn off-screen" ([Claude-Code-Game-Studios](https://github.com/Donchitos/Claude-Code-Game-Studios)). Pieter Levels' 2025 jam for AI-made games drew **1,170 entries**, and its winner was praised for responsive controls and physics that "feel right" ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/Uz0wHG4pI3KBOiFhP5YR)). The 2026 edition required at least 90% AI-written code and drew **945 entries** ([Colyseus](https://colyseus.io/blog/vibe-jam-2026/)).

This project's own record is the sharpest first-hand evidence. Building "BRO, YOU'RE COOKED." (BYC), a physics puzzle where the player draws one line, the agent shipped two unplayable builds while every automated check passed: first a line that vibrated, then one that shrank ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)). About **99 level ideas were tried and 42 shipped**, and since 13 October **44% of design time** went on levels that died. The plan promised 60 levels by 21 October and delivered 42, and the agent repeatedly drifted into building measuring tools instead of the levels the owner asked for ([Where the AI struggled](Where%20the%20AI%20struggled.md)). The project's own diagnosis fits every source above. The agent was strong wherever the machine itself could check the answer. It was weak wherever a human judgement was needed, and weakest where it mistook a simulated judgement for a real one.

Two judgements remain human. The first is **fun**. No study found shows a language model can reliably judge it. The closest result is narrower: model players do worse than people, but their results track *relative* human-reported difficulty between Wordle and Slay the Spire puzzles ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3748634)). Claude's reading of a game screen, tested on Pokémon, was still called "pretty bad" after the Opus 4.5 upgrade ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/gogZyeistdaDFuhbG/claude-plays-pokemon-opus-4-5-follow-up)). The second is **originality**. Making a level that works is cheap; making a new one is not. MarioGPT made **88%** of its levels completable when a search agent checked them ([MarioGPT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.05981)), but a model tuned with human feedback reached only **37% playable *and* novel** ([Nasir & Togelius](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.18243v3)). The authors of one whole-puzzle-game generator suspect its output is "at best a minor variation on something it has already seen" ([ScriptDoctor](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06524v1)). YouTube's own AI game maker produced games reviewers called "repetitive and uninspiring" ([Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/3d/video-game-design/you-can-now-play-ai-generated-games-in-youtube)). The dependable recipe follows from this: give the agent eyes (screenshots and filmed play), give it an exact checker, and keep a human as the judge of fun and the source of each game's twist.

## Two questions sort every genre into provable and unprovable

Two questions decide whether a computer can make and grade a game's levels on its own. The first is whether the outcome depends only on the player's separate choices, not on timing or tiny differences in input. The second is whether a computer can write down the whole state of the game. When both answers are yes, a solver gives an exact verdict ("winnable") and an exact number ("needs at least 23 moves"). When either is no, the best available is a statistical estimate, and the estimate can be wrong in ways no test notices.

**Physics games fail the first question, and this project paid for it.** A level the solver certified as won, with Milo passing just five units from death, **lost in the browser**, because a line a person redraws is never identical to the computer's ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)). Difficulty can only be estimated by sampling thousands of strokes. On BYC's hard levels the estimate rests on 8–34 winning strokes that survive a model of a shaky hand, so it carries **±15–42% noise**, about as wide as the tolerance it is checked against ([Progressive difficulty in puzzle games](Progressive%20difficulty%20in%20puzzle%20games.md)). The level generator passed 61 of 120 candidates as fair, but every one was a near-copy of an earlier level, and a later contact sheet showed all 24 fresh candidates as twins of levels 1–12 ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)). Academic generators hit the same wall. Ropossum, a Cut the Rope generator, needed a physics engine, evolutionary search and a reasoning agent, still did not strictly guarantee solvable levels, and offered "no way to influence difficulty" ([Ropossum](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12611); [follow-up](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12690)). Angry Birds generators spent years just making structures stand up, and a 2025 paper exists solely to **repair unstable generated levels** ([AIIDE 2025](https://cspages.ucalgary.ca/~richard.zhao1/publications/2025aiide-stabilizing_angry_birds_levels.pdf)).

**Grid puzzles pass both questions.** Many are labelled "NP-complete" or "PSPACE-complete", which means hard on giant boards. At the sizes a phone shows, solvers finish in under a second. Ball sort and water sort are NP-complete ([Ito et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09495v1)), yet Kociemba's public solver finds the *optimal* solution "within less than a second" for normal sizes ([Kociemba](https://github.com/hkociemba/WaterBallSortPuzzleOptimalSolver)). Sliding-block puzzles of the Rush Hour kind are PSPACE-complete, yet Michael Fogleman's MIT-licensed code solves and generates them and has mapped every interesting 6×6 puzzle ([Fogleman](https://github.com/fogleman/rush)); the hardest 6×6 start needs **51 moves** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rush_Hour_(puzzle))). One open Flow-style solver handles 40×40 grids and solves 270 published puzzles in **0.14 seconds** ([numberlink](https://github.com/thomasahle/numberlink)). Simon Tatham's MIT-licensed collection ships **40 puzzle types, each with a generator and a solver**: its Minesweeper never forces a guess, and its sudoku grades difficulty "by the complexity of the techniques" needed ([Tatham's puzzles](https://github.com/ghewgill/puzzles/blob/master/puzzles.but)). An agent has already used this approach in public. **Sortaballs**, a ball-sort game with AI-written code, ships "over 1,000 levels, each benchmarked against an optimal solver" ([awesome-ai-built-games](https://github.com/lappemic/awesome-ai-built-games)).

| Game type | Can a computer prove every level winnable? | Exact difficulty number | Free code to start from | Art needed | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Car-out parking, arrow tap-away | Yes, simply (inferred from the rules) | Depth of the blocking chain; board size | None published; simple to write | Flat shapes | **A** |
| Sliding-block unblock | Yes, by trying every move | Fewest moves (up to 51 on 6×6) | Fogleman, MIT | Flat shapes | **A** |
| Sort (water, balls, nuts on bolts) | Yes, optimally, in under a second | Fewest moves; dead ends; spare bins | Kociemba; many small solvers | Flat colours | **A** |
| Draw-a-path grids (Flow-style) | Yes | Search effort the solver needs | Solvers exist, but AGPL or non-commercial | Flat shapes | **A** |
| No-guess minesweeper, sudoku-likes, nonograms | Yes | Hardest reasoning step needed | Tatham, MIT | Flat; nonograms need pictures | **A** |
| Tile-match (triple), mahjong | Yes, if every tile is known | Spare tray slots; dead ends | None published | Icon sets | **A–B** |
| Endless block puzzle (Block Blast-style) | Each turn, yes; the piece draw is random | A survival curve, not one number | Block Blast solvers | Flat shapes | **B** |
| Match-3 | No: hard even before random refills | Win rate from many simulated plays | Research only; studios hand-make levels | Heavy | **C** |
| Physics puzzles (draw-to-save, pin-pull, rope-cutting, Angry Birds-style, Suika) | Only by sampling a simulation | A noisy estimate | Research generators that cannot steer difficulty | Physics tuning | **C–D** |
| Runners, hypercasual action, troll platformers | No: the result rests on human timing | None; playtest only | — | Heavy 3D or hand-designed tricks | **D** |

*Grade A means an agent can generate, prove and grade many levels unattended; grade D means it cannot. The arrow and car-out rows are inferred from the games' rules, because no paper or code was found for them.*

A provable genre also turns this project's three hardest-won checks from sampling jobs into exact questions. BYC needed thousands of strokes to answer each of them, and a grid puzzle answers each by re-solving.

| What BYC had to check | How BYC answered it | How a grid puzzle answers it |
|---|---|---|
| Is the new element decoration? | Delete it and re-run thousands of strokes | Remove the piece and re-solve; the same fewest-moves count means it does nothing |
| How do players actually win? | Trace every winning stroke in a sweep | List every winning solution |
| Are two levels twins? | Compare opening frames by eye | Compare the levels' solutions directly |

**Exact is not the same as human.** Only two genres have published evidence that a computed difficulty tracks what people feel. In Sokoban, features chosen in a user study "correlate strongly" with perceived difficulty ([Kartal et al.](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12859)). In match-3, King's bots predict players' pass rates ([Gudmundsson et al.](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/reinforcement-learning/imitation-learning/2018-gudmundsson.pdf)). Elsewhere the number can mislead: one open Sokoban generator admits it "cannot really distinguish if a level is difficult or not" ([Sokoban-Level-Generator](https://github.com/AlliBalliBaba/Sokoban-Level-Generator)), and no study was found that checks "fewest moves" against real players for sort, jam or arrow games. Real-player calibration is still required, but it becomes a smaller job, because the computed number has no noise of its own and only human variation remains to be measured. Two cautions follow from the games' rules; they are inferences, not findings. First, hidden pieces break the guarantee. Many commercial sort and tile games hide some pieces, and the solver may know them while the player does not, so its number stops describing what the player faces. Second, arrow and car-out difficulty has a low ceiling. If a piece leaves whenever its path is clear and removing one never blocks another, a level is winnable exactly when no pieces block each other in a loop. Depth then has to come from board size and a twist rather than from long reasoning.

## Playables crowns runners and draw puzzles; provable genres win on volume and retention

YouTube publishes no chart, but an independent tracker re-reads the public play counter across **1,939 live Playables** ([TubeGames](https://playables.games/best/)). The all-time top is studio-made 3D action: **Tall Man Run 110M**, Build a Queen 103M, Snake Clash! 99M, Stealth Master 96M and Going Balls 96M. Next come draw-input puzzles. **Thief Puzzle has 92M** and leads Brain & Puzzle, followed by Draw One Part at 41–42M, Save The Pets at about 38M, Draw Climber at about 28M and Draw Bridge at about 21M ([TubeGames Brain & Puzzle](https://playables.games/genres/brain-puzzle/); [Playables wiki](https://youtube-playables.fandom.com/wiki/Playables)). A Playables publisher calls "Draw & Escape" the strongest new mechanic of summer 2026, though it sells publishing services and is not neutral ([Mediacube](https://mediacube.io/en/blog/youtube-playables-game-mechanics)). That is the central tension for this owner: **the puzzle input with the most proven demand on Playables is the kind an agent cannot reliably build.** Not every draw hit is physics, though. Draw One Part asks for a missing piece to be drawn, not for a falling object to be steered.

Brain & Puzzle is crowded and its hits are mid-sized. The category holds **611 games and 1.1 billion combined plays**. Rough readings from a jumbled page put **Parking Rush at about 23M and Parking Jam at about 21M**, against roughly 0.7M for Hexa Sort and 0.23M for Water Sort Quest ([TubeGames](https://playables.games/genres/brain-puzzle/)). In a May 2026 scrape of 456 Playables, title counts show where the crowd sits: 23 tile and mahjong match games, 20 sort games, about 14 jam and parking games, 13 draw games, 8 arrow games, one sudoku, one minesweeper, and no nonogram or Sokoban at all ([scrape](https://github.com/CoderandHacker69/yt-playables-scraper/blob/main/youtube_playables_scraped.json)). For the provable genres, then, demand is proven, the shelves are crowded and the hits are mid-sized. Logic grids are nearly absent, which is either an opening or a sign of weak demand, and the data cannot tell which.

Mobile is where these subgenres proved themselves. In 2025 mobile puzzle games passed **9.7 billion downloads and $10 billion** in in-app spending, up 14%. Sort, screw and block puzzles were the fast-growing niches, while classic match-3 revenue was flat and word games declined ([Naavik](https://naavik.co/digest/how-niche-subgenres-are-reshaping-the-mobile-puzzle-market/)). Block Blast sat at the top of the download charts for about six months, with more than **38M installs in December 2025** alone ([mobilegamer.biz](https://mobilegamer.biz/decembers-top-mobile-game-downloads-2/)). Color Block Jam, an escape-style jam game, reached about **43M installs** by mid-2025 ([mobilegamer.biz](https://mobilegamer.biz/2025s-top-mobile-game-downloads-so-far/)). Arrows – Puzzle Escape grew downloads **130% in a month**, by one analytics firm's estimate, as near-clones filled the App Store ([Insightrackr](https://blog.insightrackr.com/en/docs/2025-December-Top-Mobile-Games)). Publishers are carrying these subgenres onto Playables, so anything new enters a crowded field.

**Retention favours puzzles, and sort games most of all.** Retention is the share of players who return: "D1" the next day, "D7" a week later. Across mobile games, GameAnalytics puts median D1 at **about 22%**, D7 at **3.4–3.9%**, and median sessions at **5–6 minutes**. Puzzle, board and card games hold players best over weeks, while arcade games lead on day one and then fade ([GameAnalytics](https://www.gameanalytics.com/reports/2025-mobile-gaming-benchmarks)). One analyst estimates queue-sort games at **52% D1, 27% D7 and 14% after a month**, far above the median ([Gamigion](https://www.gamigion.com/retention-benchmark-for-sort-puzzle-games-in-2026/)). The web portals judge early play hardest. CrazyGames says its successful titles "often see 10+ minutes" of average playtime, and it scores new games on how many players are still playing after one minute ([CrazyGames](https://docs.crazygames.com/resources/basic-launch-metrics/)). A studio featured on Poki's blog warns that if fewer than half of players pass the first minute, "the onboarding isn't good enough or the game isn't fun" ([Poki](https://poki.com/blog/developer-spotlight-blumgi-games-part-1)). At 30–60 seconds a level, a 10-minute session uses 10–20 levels, so a player who returns for a week needs hundreds. That is exactly what a solver-backed generator supplies and a hand-tuned physics game cannot. It should also be mostly easy content: in online experiments with **10,000 and 70,000 players**, people "were more engaged and played longer when the game was easier" ([Lomas et al.](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2470668)).

## Portals police duplicates, not AI, and audiences set the income

**No portal found bans games built with AI; every one polices copies.** **YouTube Playables** added a duplicate-content rule on **25 August 2026**: a Playable must not be "substantially identical" to another, and review weighs "the creator's overall history on YouTube and past Playables performance" ([Trust & Safety](https://developers.google.com/youtube/gaming/playables/certification/requirements_trustsafety); [Certification](https://developers.google.com/youtube/gaming/playables/certification/requirements)). **Poki** accepts AI-assisted games but may ask to see the tools, process and prompts. It warns that games relying "too heavily on AI-generated content or templates without adding distinct personality, polish, or innovation may not meet our standards" ([Poki: Working With AI](https://sdk.poki.com/working-with-ai.html)). **CrazyGames** has no AI rule but rejects "clones or asset flips" ([CrazyGames FAQ](https://docs.crazygames.com/faq/)). **Steam** now asks for disclosure only of AI content that players experience and exempts code assistants ([VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/valve-has-significantly-rewritten-steams-rules-for-how-developers-much-disclose-ai-use)), and **itch.io** keeps untagged AI assets off its browse pages ([GamingOnLinux](https://gamingonlinux.com/2024/11/itchio-store-now-requires-ai-generated-content-disclosures-for-assets)). The industry mood is hostile too: **52%** of developers told the GDC 2026 survey that generative AI harms the industry ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260129438528/en/2026-State-of-the-Game-Industry-Report-Reveals-Widening-Effect-of-Layoffs-Broader-Perspectives-on-Generative-AI-Unionization-Tariffs-and-More)). Two practical rules follow. A portfolio of reskins, the same game in new paint, is exactly what these policies target. And a creator who uses the agent for code but keeps the art flat or drawn by code sidesteps nearly every disclosure and ownership question.

**Getting onto Playables is slow, and the money is unclear.** There is no open sign-up; developers apply through an interest form ([Playables overview](https://developers.google.com/youtube/gaming/playables)). Publishers already on the platform quote **2–4 weeks** in exchange for a share of revenue, and one describes the direct queue as **3–6 months** with "no guarantee of approval"; both are vendor claims ([Playgama wiki](https://wiki.playgama.com/platforms/how-to-publish-your-game-on-youtube-playables); [Mediacube](https://mediacube.io/en/blog/youtube-playables-monetization)). Revenue sharing is a pilot "with a select number of Playable creators" with no published split. In-app purchases do not exist yet, and Google aims to test them during 2027 ([Monetization reference](https://developers.google.com/youtube/gaming/playables/reference/monetization); [mobilegamer.biz](https://mobilegamer.biz/youtube-has-quietly-rolled-out-playables-across-europe/)). Third-party guides report that **Poki** splits revenue 50/50 on the players it brings and asks for web exclusivity ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/publish-game-poki)). The same guides say **CrazyGames** pays an unpublished ad share above €100 and does not require exclusivity ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/publish-game-crazygames)). If those terms hold, each game must choose between Poki and the others, so check them before submitting.

**Playgama's Sandbox, launched in mid-October 2026, is the one channel built for agents.** Claude Code can publish a game to it in a single step, "without moderation". The game needs only Playgama's connector code and three cover images ([Playgama plugins](https://github.com/Playgama/playgama-plugins); [PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/playgama-launches-tools-for-ai-agents-to-publish-and-improve-games/)). Each game gets about **100 real players free**, then unlocks 200, 500 and 1,000 players if its numbers are strong, though no threshold has been published ([Kachmar, LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/dmitry-kachmar-84392725/)). Ads switch on only after an unpublished session threshold, and payouts start at **$100** ([Playgama plugins](https://github.com/Playgama/playgama-plugins)). The founder reports **1,600+ AI-built games, 420,000 launches and about 6–6.6 minutes per play**. Those figures are self-reported, and they fall short of CrazyGames' 10-minute bar. For this owner the Sandbox's value is not reach. It is the only route found that sends **real players' attempts per level back to the agent**, the missing input for any difficulty curve. BYC already has a build that reports each run's level, outcome and attempt number, waiting only for the owner's go-ahead ([docs/PLAYGAMA.md](../docs/PLAYGAMA.md)).

**An audience decides the income, and a typical small game earns little.** The famous AI-built success, Pieter Levels' flight game, earned mainly from ad slots sold to brands reaching his own large following. Its reported revenue ranges from **$5,000 to about $138,000 a month** depending on the outlet, and none of it is audited ([avgeekery](https://avgeekery.com/?p=39779); [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/2NUIOp80SUWuPmmfKoS9); [sorceress.games](https://sorceress.games/blog/real-vibe-coding-apps-what-indies-built-in-2026)). 404 Media's headline adds "yours probably won't" ([404 Media](https://404media.co/this-game-created-by-ai-vibe-coding-makes-50-000-a-month-yours-probably-wont)), and a later piece reports the revenue "went to zero" ([DEV](https://dev.to/promptway/he-built-a-flight-simulator-in-three-hours-and-hit-1m-a-year-in-17-days-then-it-went-to-zero-1b1l)). The best-earning app still on a vibe-coding host's shared address makes **$155 a month**, a floor because successful apps move to their own domains ([bigideasdb](https://bigideasdb.com/how-to-make-money-vibe-coding)). One vendor guide puts a well-performing casual web game at **$200–2,000 a month**, unverified ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/es/guides/web-game-monetization)).

The business model that fits all of this is an inference from that evidence: **one engine, many decisions, and depth inside each listing.** One codebase carries the rendering, input, saving, ads, daily puzzle, level board and certification work, and each game adds only its own rules and solver. Each game must ask the player a different decision: the sentence a player says when they get it, such as "clear the cars blocking the exit" or "fill each tube with one colour". A new coat of paint does not count. More levels and a daily challenge inside one game are safe under every duplicate rule, whereas ten near-identical games put at risk the creator history that Playables weighs. BYC's ad rules, an interstitial every third completed level ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)), make revenue grow with levels finished, which suits many short, mostly easy levels: exactly what solver-generated content supplies. The order of release follows. Test on Playgama, calibrate difficulty from real attempts, and then submit fewer, stronger games to CrazyGames or Poki, and to Playables through a publisher, with retention evidence in hand.

## The owner plays, the agent proves: a workflow that repeats

The division of labour that recurs in every successful account maps onto this owner. **The agent writes the code, the solver, the generator, the tests and the certification work, films the game, and shows its evidence.** **The owner acts as lead designer and playtester**: picking the decision each game asks, playing every build on a phone, and holding the final word over every number. Professional studios work the same way. King's bots measure thousands of levels, but "the designers decide if that is an intended experience" ([Campaign](https://www.campaignlive.co.uk/article/king-games-keeps-crushing-candy-years/1440143)). Three BYC lessons should carry into every new game from day one. Levels are the deliverable, and the agent must say plainly whenever level production stops; BYC's three-week stall began with the agent arguing that content "was never the bottleneck" ([Where the AI struggled](Where%20the%20AI%20struggled.md)). Work is committed and pushed at every checkpoint, because BYC lost an entire feature when an idle cloud machine was replaced before the work was saved ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)). And every newly discovered limit goes into one rules file the agent reads each session, rather than into thirty planning documents. The pipeline below orders the work so that the expensive human checks happen early, while they are still cheap.

| Stage | The agent does | The owner does | Done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. The decision | Proposes three twists on a proven genre, each in one sentence | Picks one by the sentence a player would say | It differs from every game already shipped |
| 2. Playable core | Builds the rules with 10 hand-made levels on the shared engine | Plays them on a phone | The owner wants to play level 11 |
| 3. Solver and generator | Writes the solver; generates levels, each stored with a winning solution | Watches a few solutions replayed on screen | Every shipped level carries its own proof |
| 4. Twin check | Removes the twist and re-solves, compares solutions, prints a grid of opening frames | Looks at the grid | No level ignores the twist; no two look alike |
| 5. Curve | Orders levels by the solver's number: a rising sawtooth with breathers, first levels easiest | Plays the first 20 and the hardest 5 | The first three need no thought; no wall appears |
| 6. Certification | Checks every screen shape, muted play, pause and saving | Reads the films of wins *and* losses | Every check passes and the films agree |
| 7. Real players | Publishes to Playgama; exports attempts, including players who quit | Approves the upload | 25–30 players per level on the levels that matter |
| 8. Calibrate and submit | Fits real attempts against the solver's number; moves outliers | Chooses the portals | The curve is stated in real attempts |

Two cautions keep the workflow honest. A provable genre removes the question "is it fair?" but not "is it meaningful?". BYC's levels A7 and A14 passed every automated check and still confused a real player ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)), which is why stage 2 and the opening-frame grid in stage 4 cannot be skipped. And a handful of friends cannot set a difficulty number. For a hard level, the error on its average attempts is roughly one divided by the square root of the number of players who won it: **±50% with 4 winners and ±20% with 25** ([Progressive difficulty in puzzle games](Progressive%20difficulty%20in%20puzzle%20games.md)). A living-room test finds walls and confusion; real numbers need stage 7.

## Ranked: what to build next, and what to leave alone

The ranking weighs four things: the platform evidence, how completely a solver can prove and grade the levels, how little art the game needs, and how crowded the shelf is. Ranks 3, 5 and 6 rest partly on inference, as the table says.

| Rank | Game type | Why it ranks here | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Jam and escape puzzles**: car-out parking and arrow tap-away for easy volume, sliding-block unblock for depth | The strongest Playables numbers of any provable genre (parking at 21–23M plays) and mobile growth (Color Block Jam, Arrows). Flat art. Levels can be built backwards so each is winnable, and MIT sliding-block code exists | Clone-filled shelves; car-out and arrow difficulty is shallow |
| **2** | **Sort puzzles**: water, balls, nuts on bolts, and queue sort with the queue visible | The best retention evidence (queue sort at 52% next-day return), a public optimal solver, an AI-built 1,000-level precedent and flat colours | The most crowded shelf; hidden-block variants break exact grading |
| **3** | **Draw-a-path grids**: Flow-style, given a story such as "draw each pet's route home" | Keeps the draw input that leads Playables' puzzle chart, and BYC's identity, with every level provable. *A bet: no play counts were found for Flow-style titles* | Weak direct evidence; solver licences force a rewrite; proving a single solution needs a separate counter |
| **4** | **Tile-match (triple)** | 23 titles in the Playables scrape and a mobile staple. Provable when every tile is known, by dealing the layout backwards from a solved order | Needs an icon set; no published generator |
| **5** | **Endless block puzzle** (Block Blast-style) | A chart-topping mobile genre with no levels to make. Difficulty is tuned by bot simulation of the piece generator, plus a daily seed | Difficulty is a curve, not a number; crowded; no level-by-level progression for ads to sit between (inference) |
| **6** | **Logic grids as a daily**: no-guess minesweeper, sudoku-likes, small nonograms | The most provable genre of all: Tatham's 40 generators, MIT-licensed and graded by technique | Nearly absent from Playables, so demand is unproven; best as a daily or a side game |

Each recommended type has its own way of making levels, a number to grade them by, and an exact check that the game's twist matters.

| Game type | How levels are made | Difficulty number | Check that the twist is real | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jam and escape | Car-out and arrow: add pieces in reverse removal order, so each level is winnable by construction. Sliding blocks: search outward from the solved board | Fewest moves (sliding); blocking-chain depth and piece count (car-out, arrow) | Neutralise the twist (a locked car, a one-way lane, a move limit) and re-solve; an unchanged fewest-moves count means decoration | Levels that look like the market leader's; trademarked names such as "Rush Hour" |
| Sort | Random starts, kept only when the optimal solver finishes them | Fewest moves, dead ends, spare bins | Re-solve without the twist | Hidden pieces; boards beyond about 14 colours by 6 per column slow the solver |
| Draw-a-path | Lay non-crossing paths, then erase all but their ends | Solver search effort, plus a solution counter for uniqueness | Remove the twist cell or rule and re-solve | Copying AGPL or non-commercial solver code |
| Tile-match | Deal backwards from a solved removal order | Spare tray slots; dead ends | Re-solve without the twist | AI-generated icons; tiles the player cannot see |
| Endless block | No levels: let a bot play thousands of games per setting of the piece generator | Survival curve per setting | Bot survival with and without the twist | Piece draws that leave no possible move |
| Logic grids | Generate, then keep only what the solver finishes (Tatham's pattern) | Hardest reasoning step needed | — | Any grid that forces a guess |

Some game types should be left alone, and the reason for each is specific.

| Avoid | Why | Exception |
|---|---|---|
| **Physics puzzles of any kind**: draw-to-save, pin-pull, rope-cutting, Angry Birds-style, Suika | Outcomes shift with a shaky hand, difficulty is a noisy estimate, and generators cannot steer difficulty or invent new-looking levels. BYC kept about 4 ideas in 10 | Ship BYC as it stands, but do not start a second physics game |
| **3D runners and hypercasual action** | They top Playables, but on studio art, reflex tuning and paid marketing, and agents score lowest on art (36.9%) and 2D graphics (33%) | None |
| **Troll platformers** (Level Devil-style) | The appeal is hand-designed surprise and timing, which no solver measures | None |
| **Match-3 and merge with an upgrade economy** | Hard to solve even before random refills, art-heavy, and built on selling boosters, which Playables cannot do without in-app purchases | None |
| **Idle and clicker games** | Economy balancing rather than levels; only 4 titles on Playables | None |
| **One-off showcase games** (flight sims, multiplayer toys) | Their earnings came from the creator's audience, not from the game | Only with an audience already in hand |
| **Reskinned portfolios** | Playables' "substantially identical" rule, CrazyGames' clone rejections and Poki's warning about templates | A new decision per game, not new paint |

## Conclusion

The evidence changes the question from "what can an agent build?" to "what can an agent *check*?" Every benchmark shows agents producing running games and falling short on content, presentation and finding their own mistakes. BYC shows the same from the inside: its gates measured fairness exactly as designed and still could not see meaning. Choosing a provable genre does not make the agent smarter. It makes fairness free and certain, so the owner's limited attention can go entirely to the one judgement no machine has been shown to make: whether a stranger enjoys the game and understands what it wants. It also answers the trust problem the owner raised about BYC's difficulty curve, because "this level needs at least 23 moves" is a fact anyone can re-run, not a model to be believed.

The commercial picture keeps the ambition honest. Provable genres are crowded, and their Playables hits are mid-sized, not blockbusters. Their advantages are retention and endless supply, which reward patience across many levels rather than a single hit launch. Whether a solo owner without an audience can earn from them is still untested, and the first useful experiment is cheap: real attempt counts from Playgama's free players. BYC's investment is not lost either. Its filming harness, opening-frame grid, daily challenge, ad rules, certification checks and difficulty-curve tooling are the half of a studio that does not care which genre runs inside it. The next game should be built on that half, with a solver where the physics used to be.
