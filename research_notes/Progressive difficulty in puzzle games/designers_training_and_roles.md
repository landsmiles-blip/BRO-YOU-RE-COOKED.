## Q1. Which degree programmes and named courses produce game and level designers, and what do they teach about difficulty, pacing and balance?

### Takeaway
Game and level designers come mostly from dedicated game-design degrees: DigiPen, USC, NYU, CMU ETC, Full Sail, Abertay, BUas (Breda), Uppsala and ITU Copenhagen. The core of these degrees is iterative prototyping and playtesting, not curve maths. Difficulty, pacing and balance show up in three kinds of course: a few dedicated "game balance" courses (Full Sail GDN4318; Ian Schreiber's course and MOOC), level-design studios that treat pacing as part of spatial design, and QA / UX / games-user-research modules. No syllabus I could see teaches a named difficulty-curve model. The curve material lives in the textbooks these courses assign (Fullerton, Schell, Schreiber/Romero), and data-driven tuning shows up only in a few newer courses, such as ITU's "Data-Driven Design and Development".

### Inferences
- Academic training teaches **process** (prototype, playtest, iterate against experience goals) far more than it teaches **models** of difficulty. The only quantitative balance content visible in curricula is Schreiber's: numeric relationships, cost curves and progression. No visible syllabus teaches pass-rate targets or attempts-per-success. Those skills are learned on the job in live free-to-play studios (see Q3).
- Level-design courses at prestige schools (NYU, DigiPen) are 3D and spatial, architecture-led and Totten-flavoured. They do not centre on puzzle-level sequencing, so the casual puzzle level designer's core skill, tuning the difficulty of thousands of small levels, has almost no academic home. That fits Playrix screening juniors by how many match-3 levels they have *played* rather than by degree (Q3).
- Graduates of these programmes expect to see games-user-research and QA/UX as separate jobs. That matters for Q4, where user research turns out to be a distinct role at large studios and absent at small ones.

### Gaps
- No DigiPen, NYU, Abertay or Uppsala syllabus listing difficulty or pacing week by week could be retrieved; their pages were blocked or only descriptions were indexed.
- I could not confirm whether the IGDA framework had a revision after 2008, or whether ACM SIGGRAPH / IGDA guidance from the 2020s exists.
- Nothing found on Chinese, Korean or Turkish programmes that feed King, Playrix, Peak and Dream Games (Istanbul). Their actual hiring pipelines may be Turkish CS/engineering degrees rather than game-design degrees (Peak hires "Product Specialists" with "no prior experience" — see Q3).

## Q2. What do the standard textbooks and canonical design writing say about building a difficulty curve, and what concrete models do they give?

### Takeaway
The canon gives designers five recurring models: (1) **the interest curve** (Schell): a hook, then a rising line of peaks and valleys, a climax at the end, and the same shape repeating at every scale ("fractal"); (2) **the flow channel / tense-and-release** (Csikszentmihalyi via Chen and Schell): keep challenge just ahead of skill and oscillate, with an easy stretch after each new skill and a hard stretch once it is learned; (3) **one skill at a time** (Cook's skill atoms and chains, Linehan et al.'s 2014 analysis of puzzle games, Nintendo's four-step *kishōtenketsu*): introduce a mechanic safely, develop it, twist it, then combine it; (4) **progression and cost curves** (Schreiber & Romero): linear, exponential, triangular and logarithmic relationships used to set how fast challenge and power grow; (5) **playtest-driven iteration** (Fullerton; Hodent for onboarding and cognitive limits). The *sawtooth* is widespread in practice (Q3), but I found no source that attributes it to Scott Rogers' *Level Up!*.

### Cited Findings
**Jesse Schell, *The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses* (1st ed. 2008; 3rd ed. CRC/Routledge 2019)**
- The interest curve is **Lens #61, "The Lens of the Interest Curve"**, in the chapter on judging experiences by their interest curves (Ch. 17 in the summary's edition). It is about pacing. Dan Cook's review of the book reproduces Schell's illustration of a "fractal interest curve". [M] — [Game Developer: book review (Dan Cook)](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/book-review-the-art-of-game-design); [Routledge 3rd ed.](https://www.routledge.com/The-Art-of-Game-Design-A-Book-of-Lenses-Third-Edition/Schell/p/book/9781138632059)
- The ideal shape, per secondary summaries: "an initial peak followed by a gradual increase (with peaks and valley) that increase until the final climax at the end of the experience", i.e. the hook, rising peaks, strategic valleys and a climax. A critique paraphrases Schell's juggling story as a curve that "begins with a catchy intro, wiggles its way up and down as it moves right, and ends quickly after a last big-bang". [L] — [Notes by Lex: Interest Curve](https://notesbylex.com/interest-curve); [Game Developer critique](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/is-this-the-best-we-ve-got-a-critique-of-tech-crunch-s-game-design)
- The lens's questions (as relayed by a course page): is there "a grand finale, more interesting than everything else?"; "is there a fractal structure to my interest curve?"; "If I ask playtesters to draw an interest curve, what does it look like?" The last one makes the curve a **playtest instrument**, not just a plan. [L] — [CGDC course page](https://cgdc.org/?p=3902)
- Schell's flow material, per a Game Developer article that attributes it to him: an "oscillation" of tense and release. Give the player a new skill or weapon while easing difficulty, raise the difficulty once they are comfortable, then lower it again. The rule underneath is never to introduce a new skill without letting the player adjust to it. Anxiety comes when challenge outruns skill, boredom when skill outruns challenge. [L — secondary interpretation] — [Game Developer: Understanding the flow channel](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/understanding-the-flow-channel-in-game-design)
- An e-learning adaptation describes Schell's curve as "the flow of events over time" whose first job is to lift initial interest ("the hook") before settling "down to business". [L] — [Karl Kapp: Does your e-learning have an interest curve?](https://karlkapp.com/?p=1653)

**Tracy Fullerton, *Game Design Workshop* (1st ed. 2004 with Swain & Hoffman; 5th ed. CRC 2024)**
- It is "playcentric": set **player experience goals**, prototype, playtest, evaluate against the goals, and "this process is repeated until solutions are found and the design is deemed to meet the goals". The loop applies "from initial conception through final quality assurance testing". [M] — [Game Developer book excerpt](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/book-excerpt-game-design-workshop); [Routledge 5th ed.](https://www.routledge.com/Game-Design-Workshop-A-Playcentric-Approach-to-Creating-Innovative-Games/Fullerton/p/book/9781032607009)
- In the 5th edition, *Playtesting* is Chapter 9 (37 pp.) in Part 2, after Ideation, Prototyping and Digital Prototyping. The 2004 edition's playtesting chapter frames it as "a process which involves selection, recruiting, preparation, controls, and analysis". [M] — [GlobalSpec: Chapter 8 Playtesting (2004 ed.)](https://www.globalspec.com/reference/35428/203279/chapter-8-playtesting); [UK library record, 5th ed.](https://www.aspire.hud.ac.uk/product/9781032607009/game-design-workshop-a-playcentric-approach-to-creating-innovative-games-the-end)
- It is the textbook for USC's required CTIN 488 and is assigned at MIT CMS.608 (Q1). [H]

**Ian Schreiber & Brenda Romero, *Game Balance* (CRC Press / A K Peters, 2021; 806 pp.)**
- The publisher defines game balance as the process of making a game "simultaneously fair for players while providing them just the right amount of difficulty", drawing on mathematics, psychology, economics and game theory. [H] — [Routledge: Game Balance](https://www.routledge.com/Game-Balance/Schreiber-Romero/p/book/9781498799577); [Taylor & Francis](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/9781315156422)
- Confirmed chapters include "Transitive Mechanics and Cost Curves", "Situational Balance", **"Progression in PvE Games"**, "Progression in PvP Games", "Analytics", "Metagame Systems" and "Beyond Balance". **No chapter titled "Difficulty Curves" was found.** A third-party summary describes a chapter "Progress Curves, or How Growth Feels" and a theme of "why players feel balance, not see it". [M for chapter list / L for summary] — [British Library record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/020203409); [XP Library summary](https://vasiuk.substack.com/p/game-balance)
- The curve vocabulary, via teaching material built on Schreiber's 2010 *Game Balance Concepts* course: **linear** relationships, **exponential** relationships, **triangular** numbers (1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, 28; differences grow linearly, so the total is quadratic) and periodic relationships. Logarithmic curves are described elsewhere as the inverse of exponential ones. A newsletter applying these ideas: exponential costs make "early levels cheap and later ones expensive", which gives early players quick progress, and the curves are "starting points, not fixed rules". [M/L] — [UBC CPSC 427 slides](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rhodin/2020_2021_CPSC_427/lectures/14_BalancingGames.pdf); [UserWise: Mathematics of game balance](https://blog.userwise.io/blog/the-mathematics-of-game-balance); [Signposted #12](https://signposted.beehiiv.com/p/issue-12)
- The chapter list also places "Progression in PvE Games" near "Managing Luck and Skill" and "Human Intuition". [M] — [Routledge listing](https://www.routledge.com/9781498799577)
- **Not verified (two searches returned only tables of contents):** the exact way Schreiber & Romero define perceived difficulty, or plot a PvE difficulty curve. My recollection, not confirmed here, is that they separate player skill, player power and challenge. A writer should mark this as unsourced unless the book is checked.

**Raph Koster, *A Theory of Fun for Game Design* (2004/2005; 10th-anniversary ed. 2013)**
- "Fun is just another word for learning": fun is the brain's reward for learning patterns. A game stops being fun when it is too simple *or* too complex for the player, and once mastered (the tic-tac-toe example) it "no longer holds appeal". Fun is "bumping against the top of what you can do and maybe even moving the margin up". [M] — [Bumbling Through Dungeons review](https://bumblingthroughdungeons.com/theory-fun-game-design-raph-koster/); [Engadget: Koster's GDC Online 2012 keynote](https://www.engadget.com/2012-10-10-gdc-online-2012-raph-kosters-keynote-on-a-theory-of-fun-10-ye.html)
- Ian Bogost criticised its focus on fun (*Unit Operations*, ch. 8). [L] — same review.
- Koster's atoms are *nested*, unlike Cook's *chains* (see below). [M] — [Raph Koster: Cooking up Chemistry (2007)](https://www.raphkoster.com/2007/07/23/cooking-up-chemistry/)

**Daniel Cook (Lostgarden; Spry Fox)**
- *The Chemistry of Game Design* (Lostgarden / Gamasutra, July 2007): **skill atoms** are self-contained feedback loops built from tokens, verbs, rules and aesthetics, each describing how a player gains one new skill. Atoms link into **skill chains** (basic skills before advanced ones), a "system for visually mapping out how skills are mastered throughout a game". [H] — [Lostgarden 2007](https://lostgarden.com/2007/07/19/the-chemistry-of-game-design/); [Game Developer version](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-chemistry-of-game-design)
- The 2021 repost renames the atom an **interaction loop** and adds a **Decision** step: the player observes known affordances and weighs outcomes before acting. [M] — [Lostgarden 2021](https://lostgarden.com/2021/03/13/the-chemistry-of-game-design-2/)
- *Loops and Arcs* (April 2012): a **loop** runs intent → action → system response → feedback → updated mental model, and loops are "fractal", occurring at several levels and frequencies. An **arc** has the same structure but does not repeat (story-like content). Commentators apply it to puzzle games such as Monument Valley, where puzzle-solving is the loop and the run from simple teaching puzzles to hard ones is the arc. [M] — [Lostgarden: Loops and Arcs](https://lostgarden.com/2012/04/30/loops-and-arcs); [Mechanics of Magic sketchnote](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/?p=55553)

**Christopher Totten, *An Architectural Approach to Level Design* (1st ed. 2014; 2nd ed. A K Peters/CRC 2019)**
- The 2019 contents include **"Pacing Your Levels with the Nintendo Power Method"** (a workflow taken from the magazine), iterative design with playtesting, level design scheduling, "Pacing and Narrative Rewards", "reward spaces" and "goals and reward schedules", and building tutorial levels. It is described as one of the first books to bring architectural and spatial theory into level design. The method's steps were not retrievable. [M] — [British Library 2019 record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/019419773); [DiGRA book notice](https://digra.org/book-an-architectural-approach-to-level-design/)

**Scott Rogers, *Level Up! The Guide to Great Video Game Design* (1st ed. 2010; 2nd ed. 2014; 3rd ed. Wiley 2024, 576 pp.)**
- 2nd edition: "Writing the GDD, Step 3: Gameplay Progression" (p. 81), then **"Step 4: The Beat Chart"** (p. 82). 3rd edition: a section "You've Got the Beat" (p. 240) inside "Level 9: Everything I learned about level design, I learned from Level 9". [M] — [Packt: Level Up 2nd ed. contents](https://subscription.packtpub.com/book/game-development/9781118877166/15); [Wiley 3rd ed.](https://www.wiley-vch.de/de/fachgebiete/computer-und-informatik/level-up-the-guide-to-great-video-game-design-978-1-394-29876-1)

**Celia Hodent, *The Gamer's Brain* (CRC 2017) and GDC 2016 "The Gamer's Brain, Part 2: UX of Onboarding and Player Engagement"**
- The book defines pillars of **usability** ("ease of use") and **engage-ability** ("the ability of the game to be fun and engaging"), turned into a checklist. It covers perception, memory, attention, motivation, emotion and learning. The GDC talk covered onboarding pitfalls and guidelines "based on how the brain learns", using Epic and Fortnite as examples. Her later GDC masterclass covers the brain's limits (perception, attention, memory), learning principles "to onboard players efficiently", and "gameflow". Specific claims about cognitive load were not retrievable. [M] — [GDC Vault: The Gamer's Brain Part 2](https://gdcvault.com/play/1022951/The-Gamer-s-Brain-Part); [GDC masterclass listing](https://schedule.gdconf.com/session/game-ux-mindset-masterclass-principles-and-methods/914314); [Goodreads author blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/21085992-the-gamer-s-brain-part-2-ux-of-onboarding-and-player-engagement-gdc16?tab=author)

**Other canonical writing on difficulty progression**
- **Kishōtenketsu / Nintendo's four-step level design** (Koichi Hayashida, *Super Mario 3D World*, c. 2013): introduce a mechanic somewhere safe (*ki*), develop it and raise the stakes (*shō*), complicate it unexpectedly (*ten*), then bring the earlier ideas together (*ketsu*). Mostly documented through video essays and interviews rather than Nintendo's own writing. [M] — [MCV: Nintendo's level design secrets in four steps](https://www.mcvuk.com/development/video-nintendos-level-design-secrets-in-four-steps); [Kotaku: What made Super Mario 3D World so great](https://kotaku.com/what-made-super-mario-3d-world-so-great-1691916400); [Wikipedia: Koichi Hayashida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koichi_Hayashida)
- **Linehan, Bellord, Kirman, Morford & Roche, "Learning curves: analysing pace and challenge in four successful puzzle games"** (CHI PLAY 2014, pp. 181–190, doi 10.1145/2658537.2658695). Hunicke, "The case for dynamic difficulty adjustment in games", ACE 2005, pp. 429–433. [M] — [Hamlet PDF](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/pubs/Hamlet.pdf); [IxDF: Robin Hunicke](https://ixdf.org/literature/author/robin-hunicke)

### Inferences
- Across the canon the dominant **macro shape** is the same: rising overall, with local oscillation and the hardest point near the end of each unit (Schell's fractal interest curve, Schell's tense and release, kishōtenketsu's twist, Rogers' beat chart). The dominant **micro rule** is also the same: one new thing at a time, first alone, then combined (Cook, Linehan et al., Hayashida). Casual studios' "sawtooth" and "one hard level in four or five" (Q3) is the free-to-play, metric-driven version of the same idea.
- The texts are qualitative about difficulty *level*. The only quantitative curve maths in the canon (Schreiber & Romero) is about growth of numbers (costs, power, XP), not about how hard a puzzle is. Measuring puzzle difficulty as a pass rate is an industry practice, and its academic home is games-AI and HCI papers (Roohi et al. 2020; Kristensen et al. 2024, see Q3), not design textbooks.
- Schell's "ask playtesters to draw the interest curve" and Fullerton's experience goals are the canon's answer to "how do you know the curve is right?". Both are playtest-based, not data-based.

### Gaps
- No verbatim text from any of these books could be read: every publisher, Lostgarden and Game Developer page was blocked. The Schell curve's labelled points, Fullerton's playtester taxonomy, Schreiber & Romero's PvE difficulty-curve figures and Totten's Nintendo Power method steps all still need checking against the books.
- The origin of the term "sawtooth difficulty curve" was not found. It should not be attributed to Rogers.
- I did not search for Schell's "Lens of Challenge" / "Lens of Flow" wording, Adams' *Fundamentals of Game Design*, or Anna Anthropy & Naomi Clark's *A Game Design Vocabulary*, which also cover teaching through level sequence.

## Q3. How do level designers at casual and mobile puzzle studios work day to day, how do they estimate difficulty before release, and what do real job postings ask for?

### Takeaway
At King, Rovio, Playrix, Voodoo, SayGames and the like, the **level designer** builds levels in an in-house editor, tunes each one to a target difficulty, and then **re-tunes live levels from player data indefinitely**. Before release, difficulty is estimated by human playtesting and, more and more, by **AI playtest bots trained on player move data** (King) or **reinforcement-learning agents plus population simulators** (Rovio with Aalto University), which predict pass rate and churn. After release, the working metrics are **pass/win rate, attempts per success, fail rate, churn at a level, booster use, and monetisation on failure**, tested with long A/B tests. Postings consistently ask for (a) many hours of playing the genre, (b) a "solid grasp of game balancing and pacing", and (c) comfort reading data and working with BI and data science. Big studios separate data science, user research and live-ops. Hypercasual and small studios fold them into one "game & level designer".

### Inferences
- The day-to-day craft has two halves. **Authoring** (concept → layout in an editor → self-test → bot or peer test → tune to a target band) and **live re-tuning** (read pass rate, attempts, churn and spend per level, then adjust or replace, often under A/B). King calls the second half a loop that "goes on forever". Rovio budgets it as weekly content goals.
- The working difficulty unit in industry is **attempts per success** (or its inverse, pass rate), measured on real players or predicted by human-like bots. That is the same quantity this project's "tries = 1 / (breadth × hand rate)" estimates by simulation. King's bots and Roohi et al.'s agents are the industrial version of the same idea, and both insist on modelling **human-like** players at several skill levels, not optimal solvers.
- Industry cadence (first hard level around 10; one hard level every 4–5; easy after hard) matches the canon's tense and release, and closely mirrors this project's own ten-slot sawtooth with its first boss at level 10. That is external support for the shape, not proof of the exact numbers.
- Junior hiring screens for **genre play hours** (Playrix's 500-level bar) and "balancing and pacing" sense rather than degrees, which fits Q1's finding that degrees teach process, not puzzle-difficulty craft.

## Q4. Is difficulty tuning treated as a craft, a data-science task or both, and which roles own the difficulty-curve document?

### Takeaway
It is **both, with a clear division of labour at scale**. Level designers (and at Rovio, the **lead level designer**) own the difficulty curve as a design artefact: the framework of target difficulty per slot, the mechanic introduction order, where the hard levels go. **Data science and analytics** own how success is measured (engagement models, funnels, A/B design) and push designers to judge a level within the whole progression. **User research** checks comprehension and onboarding. **Economy and live-ops designers** own currencies, boosters and events, which change effective difficulty. At hypercasual and hybrid-casual studios, all of this is usually one "game & level designer" working with a data analyst. AI bots are moving pre-release estimation from designers to tooling, but in every account I found the **designer still sets the criteria**.

### Cited Findings
- **Lead level designer owns the curve framework**: Rovio's Lead Level Designer defines "clear frameworks for difficulty curves and power-up systems … to help the team design with precision", reviews daily player data, and works with BI/data teams and the content producer. [H] — [Lever: Rovio Lead Level Designer](https://jobs.lever.co/rovio-2/a7f7688d-faa3-4de3-923c-dcae8ddcaac7)
- **Individual level designers own per-level tuning and the curve across levels**: Voodoo ("tune difficulty curves"), CoolGames ("tuning difficulty curves"), CrazyLabs ("define and improve level progression, sequencing, pacing, difficulty curves"), King ("building, assessing, rebalancing and tuning … analyzing real-time feedback and metrics"). ### Inferences
- The "difficulty curve document" in mature F2P puzzle studios is best seen as a **framework owned by the lead level designer** (target difficulty bands per slot, hard-level cadence, mechanic introduction order, booster and power-up assumptions), **validated and challenged by data science** (funnels, churn per level, long A/B tests) and **constrained by economy/live-ops** (boosters, rewarded ads, events change effective difficulty). No source I found names a single "difficulty curve document" owner across studios. This is a synthesis.
- Its shape follows the canon (Schell, Cook, kishōtenketsu). Its numbers come from data (pass rate, attempts, churn). The textbooks teach the first half and almost nothing about the second, which is why studios train level designers in-house and hire from genre players.
- The trend from 2024 to 2026 is towards **AI-predicted difficulty before release** (King bots; Rovio/Aalto agents plus population models) and fewer human playtest and re-tune hours (King's reported 95% fewer manual adjustments, and the 2025 restructuring reports). The designer's role moves towards setting criteria and owning the curve framework, not testing each level by hand.
- For a small project with no live players (like this one), the closest equivalent is: the designer owns the curve; a simulated human-like player model stands in for both the data scientist and the bot; and real playtests stand in for user research. That is the split this repository's CLAUDE.md already describes, with "playtesting outranks the suite" matching the industry's position that bots inform the designer but do not replace judgement.

### Gaps
- No org chart or GDC talk was found that states outright, for King, Playrix, Dream Games or Peak, who signs off the difficulty curve (lead level designer, game director or product manager).
- How user researchers work on difficulty specifically (comprehension tests of new blockers, onboarding studies) at mobile puzzle studios was not documented in anything retrievable.
- The roles of economy and live-ops designers in difficulty (for example, a booster economy deliberately set against hard-level frequency) are inferred from job titles, not from a documented process.
