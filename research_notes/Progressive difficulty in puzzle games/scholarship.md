## 1. Flow theory and games: what should the curve look like?

### Takeaway
Flow-based game design (Chen 2007; Sweetser & Wyeth 2005) says challenge should track skill inside a "flow channel", and that the channel differs between players. Controlled studies since 2016 add something the original theory did not say: **constant balance is not optimal.** Demand that rises and falls (peaks with short breaks) produces more flow and motivation than a flat match. Separately, when players do not choose the difficulty, easier versions keep them playing longer (Lomas).

### Cited Findings
- Chen, "Flow in Games (and Everything Else)", *Communications of the ACM* 50(4):31–34, April 2007. The abstract: "A well-designed game transports its players to their personal Flow Zones, delivering genuine feelings of pleasure and happiness." — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F1232743.1232769); [PDF copy](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/~lieber/courses/csu670/f08/materials/p31-chen-flow-in-games.pdf)
- Chen frames the flow zone as a balance between challenge and ability. Challenge beyond ability "generates anxiety"; challenge that fails to engage leads the player to "quickly lose interest and tend to leave". His figures show that **novice and hardcore players have different flow zones**, and that designers adapt flow "through the choices they deliberately build into the experience" (embedded, player-driven difficulty choice). — [search summary of the CACM article](https://khoury.northeastern.edu/~lieber/courses/csu670/f08/materials/p31-chen-flow-in-games.pdf)
- Chen on *hidden* DDA (2006 interview, not peer-reviewed): players "felt cheated" when they noticed a game secretly adjusting, and he called that "the wrong way to implement DDA". He preferred difficulty controlled by the player, as in flOw, where the player chooses which plane to dive into and what to eat. — [Joystiq interview via Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2006-09-18-joystiq-qanda-jenova-chen.html); [Wikipedia: flOw](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/FlOw)
- Sweetser & Wyeth's GameFlow (2005, *ACM Computers in Entertainment*) maps Csikszentmihalyi's flow onto **eight elements** of player enjoyment. Its Challenge element requires games to be "sufficiently challenging and match the player's skill level". — [GameFlow PDF (NTNU copy)](https://ntnu.no/wiki/download/attachments/112986425/GameFlow.pdf?api=v2); [2013 revisit, J. Creative Technologies](https://ojs.aut.ac.nz/journal-of-creative-technologies/article/view/16)
- GameFlow's Challenge criteria, per copies of the criteria table: challenge should **increase as the player progresses and gains skill**; games should provide **new challenges at an appropriate pace**; and (in a later version) games should offer different levels of challenge for different players. The wording differs slightly between copies. — [Lille copy of Sweetser & Wyeth 2005](https://fabrique-formations.univ-lille.fr/fileadmin/user_upload/learning-fab/Documents/Echelles_REFA/Autotelisme-flow/GameFlow_Sweeter_2005.pdf); [NTNU course copy](https://ntnu.no/wiki/download/attachments/197920535/GameFlow.pdf?api=v2) (from search summaries of those PDFs; exact wording not quoted)
- The 2013 revisit (Sweetser, Johnson, Wyeth) concedes that the general criteria are less implementable than detailed low-level heuristics, and gives 165 of them for real-time strategy games. — [J. Creative Technologies](https://ojs.aut.ac.nz/journal-of-creative-technologies/article/view/16)
- **Baumann, Lürig & Engeser (2016)**, "Flow and enjoyment beyond skill-demand balance: The role of game pacing curves and personality", *Motivation and Emotion* 40:507–519. They compared constant balance against two *dynamic* pacing conditions in which demand rose and fell with short breaks: "dynamic medium" (no overload) and "dynamic high" (slight overload). **Constant balance gave neither the most flow nor the most enjoyment.** Flow was higher under both dynamic conditions, and enjoyment peaked under dynamic high. Personality moderated this: action orientation kept flow high even under constant balance, and sensation seeking raised enjoyment under dynamic high. — [author PDF, Uni Trier](https://www.uni-trier.de/fileadmin/fb1/prof/PSY/PGA/unterlagen/BaumannL%C3%BCrigEngeser_Flow_MOEM_2016.pdf)
- Lomas, Patel, Forlizzi & Koedinger, CHI 2013. A test of the inverted-U hypothesis in an online maths game, run as two experiments of **10K and 70K players** (2x3 and 2x9x8x4x25 factorial designs). "In almost all cases, subjects were more engaged and played longer when the game was **easier**." The most engaging conditions also produced the slowest learning. — [DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2470668); [PDF copy](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/463-663/project-part-one/Optimizing%20Challenge.pdf)
- Lomas, Forlizzi, Koedinger et al., CHI 2017, "Is Difficulty Overrated?", across more than 20,000 sessions. Players who **chose** their own difficulty were most motivated at moderate levels; with difficulty **randomly assigned**, the easiest version was most motivating. Moderate novelty was best, and suspense helped in "close games". Recommendation: "easy but interesting". — [CMU HCII summary](https://hcii.cmu.edu/news/chi-preview-researchers-hcii-believe-difficulty-can-be-overrated-games)
- A 2023 PLOS ONE study (Icy Tower) found that involvement rises with difficulty while the task is feasible and "drops rapidly when a task is so difficult that it cannot be completed". — [PMC10004480](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10004480/)

### Inferences
- Flow theory, read honestly, prescribes a *band* (a channel) and not a line. The 2016+ evidence says to *oscillate* inside or slightly above that band rather than hold it flat. That supports a sawtooth with breathers, and it does not support a monotone ramp.
- The Lomas results warn that for a game where players do not pick their level, erring easy costs less engagement than erring hard. Combined with the Icy Tower result, the asymmetry is clear: a level that is *impossible-feeling* collapses involvement, while one that is slightly too easy does not.
- Chen's "different players have different flow zones" is the theoretical reason a single static curve can never fit everyone. It also argues for player-chosen escape valves (Chen's embedded choice) over hidden adjustment.

### Gaps
- The GameFlow Challenge criteria are confirmed only through search summaries of hosted copies, not read directly. Check the PDF before quoting exact wording.
- No primary-text access to Chen's CACM figures, so the exact shape of his flow-zone diagram, and whether it shows oscillation inside the channel, is unverified.
- Csikszentmihalyi's original channel model was not searched directly; it is cited here only through Chen and Sweetser & Wyeth.

## 2. Difficulty as probability of failure: Aponte, Levieux & Natkin

### Takeaway
The CNAM group (Aponte, Levieux, Natkin; ICEC 2009, ACE 2011, *Entertainment Computing* 2011) defines objective difficulty as **the probability that a player fails a challenge**. It is estimated statistically after splitting gameplay into measurable challenges and accounting for the player's learning over time. They split difficulty into sensory, logical and motor kinds. Later work shows that players' *perceived* difficulty diverges from it: players strongly underestimate high difficulty. Later still, ~50% success is framed as "optimal".

### Cited Findings
- Aponte, Levieux & Natkin, "Scaling the Level of Difficulty in Single Player Video Games", ICEC 2009 (Springer LNCS, pp. 24–35). It seeks a general, precise, measurable definition of difficulty and links tension–resolution cycles to enjoyment. — [Springer listing](https://www.springerprofessional.de/scaling-the-level-of-difficulty-in-single-player-video-games/3189896)
- "Measuring the level of difficulty in single player video games", *Entertainment Computing* 2011. It splits gameplay into measurable items and factors in the player's learning curve to estimate difficulty statistically. Tested on an FPS, "a very simple version of our model can predict the probability [that] the player has to lose with enough accuracy to be useful as a game design tool". However, "the subjective feeling of difficulty seems to be complex". — [CNAM record](https://cedric.cnam.fr/index2.php/publis/article/ALN11); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1875952111000231)
- The group treats **probability of success as "the principal and most observable aspect of difficulty"**. It defines difficulty as a function of player experience and of time, the first function giving "the difficulty of the challenge in terms of the probability that a player overcomes it". — [search summary of the ACE 2011 / EC 2011 papers](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2071423.2071484)
- Three kinds of difficulty: **sensory** (effort to acquire information about game state), **logical** (effort to work out the solution) and **motor** (execution). Objective difficulty is estimated from players' successes and failures and kept distinct from *perceived* difficulty. — [search summary, "Difficulty in Videogames: an Experimental Validation of a Formal Definition", ACE 2011](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2071423.2071484)
- Estimation method in the group's later work: objective difficulty per challenge "was estimated using a **mixed effects logistic regression**". The challenge's time and difficulty parameters were the fixed effects, plus random intercepts per player. — [search summary of Constant, Levieux, Buendia & Natkin, INTERACT 2017](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-67684-5_8); [CNAM PDF](https://cedric.cnam.fr/fichiers/art_3969.pdf)
- Constant et al. (INTERACT 2017), "From Objective to Subjective Difficulty Evaluation". They found "a gap between the players' actual and self-evaluated chances of failure". Players "strongly underestimate high levels of difficulty", and estimation error showed no influence of gender, self-efficacy, risk aversion or gaming habits. — [IFIP record](https://dl.ifip.org/IFIP-LNCS/hal-01678476v1); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-67684-5_8)
- Constant & Levieux (CHI 2019, "Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment Impact on Players' Confidence") is described by later work as conceptualising "optimal difficulty" as **close to a 50% chance of succeeding**. — [ACM full text page](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3290605.3300693) (secondhand via search summary)

**Equation (mixed-effects logistic difficulty model) [my formalisation of the described method]:**
For player *i* on challenge *j*, with challenge covariates **x**_j (its design parameters and elapsed time or attempt number):

    logit P(fail_ij) = β0 + β·x_j + u_i,    u_i ~ Normal(0, σ²)

The player random intercept u_i absorbs individual skill. Objective difficulty of challenge *j* for an average player is then logistic(β0 + β·x_j).

### Inferences
- This project's split of TRIES into **breadth** (share of plausible strokes that win) and **hand rate** (share a shaky hand still lands) lines up closely with Aponte et al.'s **logical vs motor** difficulty. Breadth approximates logical/search difficulty and hand rate approximates motor difficulty. Neither measures sensory difficulty, which is "can a stranger read the frozen frame". The project already found that one by eye (A20, SINK). The literature names it as a separate axis, which supports keeping a human-look gate alongside the numeric ones.
- The "players underestimate high difficulty" finding suggests a measured spike will *feel* less hard in the moment than it is. The cost may then show up as quitting rather than as complaints.
- The ~50% framing sits on a per-attempt success probability. This game's per-stroke probabilities are far lower (breadth 2–40% times hand rate), so "50%" would have to apply to some aggregate, such as success within N attempts, and not to a single stroke.

### Gaps
- The exact 2009/2011 formulas, including the time-dependent difficulty function and its learning term, could not be seen. Only the prose descriptions above are verified.
- I could not confirm whether the 2011 FPS validation reported a specific accuracy figure.

## 3. Learning curves in puzzle games: Linehan et al. 2014 and follow-ups

### Takeaway
Linehan, Bellord, Kirman, Morford & Roche (CHI PLAY 2014) analysed play-through videos of Portal, Portal 2 (co-op), Braid and Lemmings with behavioural-psychology problem-solving metrics. All four games: introduce each skill **separately**, start with puzzles needing only basic use of it, give room to **practise and combine** it with earlier skills, and raise complexity until the next skill arrives. Follow-ups turned this into an evaluation method (Scozzi et al. 2017) and an automated progression generator (Butler et al. 2015).

### Cited Findings
- Linehan, Bellord, Kirman, Morford & Roche, "Learning curves: analysing pace and challenge in four successful puzzle games", CHI PLAY 2014, pp. 181–190, DOI 10.1145/2658537.2658695. Method: play-through videos of Portal, Portal 2 co-op, Braid and Lemmings, analysed with metrics from behavioural psychology's account of problem-solving. — [CORA (UCC)](https://cora.ucc.ie/handle/10468/3461); [UCC research portal](https://research.ucc.ie/en/publications/learning-curves-analysing-pace-and-challenge-in-four-successful-p/)
- Findings, from the abstract: each game (1) introduces its main skills separately, (2) starts with simple puzzles requiring only basic use of the new skill, (3) gives players room to practise and to combine it with previously learned skills, and (4) raises puzzle complexity until the next skill is introduced. Offered as practical design guidance. — [CORA (UCC)](https://cora.ucc.ie/handle/10468/3461)
- Follow-up: Scozzi, Iacovides & Linehan, "A Mixed Method Approach for Evaluating and Improving the Design of Learning in Puzzle Games", CHI PLAY 2017, pp. 217–228, DOI 10.1145/3116595.3116628. It combines quantitative and experiential methods and **compares optimal puzzle solutions with what players actually did**, to find where learning breaks down and suggest redesigns. Tested on Portal with **14 participants** of varying expertise. — [Open Research Online](https://oro.open.ac.uk/50719); [York eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/130552)
- Butler, Andersen, Smith, Gulwani & Popović, "Automatic Game Progression Design through Analysis of Solution Features", CHI 2015, pp. 2407–2416. It borrows intelligent-tutoring ideas to generate level progressions aimed at mastery of both **base concepts and their combinations**. The input is a model of what a player must do in each level, and difficulty is tunable by high-level parameters. On a math puzzle game with **2,377 players**, its progressions were "comparable to expert-crafted ones after a few design iterations" on an engagement metric. — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=334439); [researchr](https://researchr.org/publication/ButlerASGP15/authors)
- van Kreveld, Löffler & Mutser, "Automated Puzzle Difficulty Estimation", IEEE CIG 2015, pp. 415–422. It combines level features (e.g. size) into one difficulty function fitted to a **small user study's ratings**, and tests it on Flow, Lazors and Move. Mean error was about **1 point on a 1–10 scale** for Lazors and Move and **under 0.5** for Flow. "A small number of puzzles" need judging by play-testers, after which any number can be scored automatically. — [author page](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~loffl001/publications/html/Automated_Puzzle_Difficulty_Estimation); [Utrecht project page](https://www.uu.nl/en/research/algorithms/geometric-computing/research-themes/analysis-of-games-and-puzzles/puzzle-difficulty-estimation)

### Inferences
- Linehan's four patterns map almost one to one onto this project's rules: "a new NOUN, introduced alone before it is ever combined"; "a new thing's first level must stay its easiest"; "the combine tier". The literature backs the rule. It also adds a step the project does not explicitly track: a **practice** phase. After a skill is introduced there are more levels using it *alone*, getting harder, before it is combined.
- Butler et al.'s framing ("what must the player do in each level" mapped to concepts and combinations) is the academic version of this project's DECISION sentence ("no two levels ask for the same decision"). Their generator treats the progression as a curriculum over concept combinations. That is a way to *plan* the 60-slot build list by decision coverage and not only by difficulty target.
- Scozzi et al.'s "compare optimal solutions against what players actually do" is the method behind this project's census and certified-winner tracing, applied to humans rather than to the solver.

### Gaps
- I could not see Linehan et al.'s actual measured pacing numbers (e.g. puzzles per skill, gap before combination), so no quantitative "how many practice levels" figure is available from this paper.
- I found no paper replicating Linehan's analysis on a *physics* puzzle game (Cut the Rope, Angry Birds, draw-a-line games).

## 4. Juul on failure, retries and perceived fairness

### Takeaway
Juul's player study ("Fear of Failing?", 2009) found that players **prefer feeling responsible for their failures**, and that failure works by making players *readjust their understanding* of the game. *The Art of Failure* (2013) names the "paradox of failure": players willingly enter an activity where they will feel inadequate, and the escape from that inadequacy, usually by improving, is the core pleasure.

### Cited Findings
- Juul, "Fear of Failing? The Many Meanings of Difficulty in Video Games", in *The Video Game Theory Reader 2* (2009). The study "indicates that failure serves the deeper function of making players readjust their perception of a game". Failure adds to the experience by letting players notice new nuances, not merely by making winning sweeter. — [author's page](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)
- Same essay: "players clearly prefer feeling responsible for failing in a game"; not feeling responsible is tied to a negative perception of the game. — [author's page](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)
- Secondary coverage adds that players sometimes **handicap themselves** when a game becomes too easy. — [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/neuropsych/play-video-games-to-fail/) (popular press; lower confidence)
- *The Art of Failure* (MIT Press, 2013): game players "choose to engage in an activity in which they are nearly certain to fail and feel incompetent". "When you fail in a game, you (not a character) are in some way inadequate." Games "motivate us to play more, in order to escape that inadequacy". Juul rejects catharsis: games "do not purge us of unpleasant emotions; they produce them in the first place". — [book site](https://jesperjuul.net/artoffailure/about.html); [MIT CMS talk page](https://cms.mit.edu/jesper-juul-the-pain-of-playing-video-games)
- Attribution matters in telemetry too: Allart et al. (FDG 2017) explain a divergence between Rayman Legends and The Division in early play through attribution theory. In Rayman, failure is attributed to the player's own skill; in The Division the avatar can be strengthened quickly. — [CNAM record](https://cedric.cnam.fr/index2.php/publis/article/ALP17)

### Inferences
- "Players prefer feeling responsible for failure" is the theoretical basis for this project's rule that the death cam must *explain* the loss. It is also why a non-deterministic stroke outcome is corrosive: the A20 case, where the certified winner won in the solver and lost in the browser. A loss the player cannot attribute to their own choice is the bad kind of failure in Juul's terms.
- "Failure makes players readjust their perception" means a failed attempt only has value if it teaches something about the level. That is another argument for the hint naming the PROBLEM, and for the failure replay showing the mechanism.

### Gaps
- Juul's 2009 study sample size and method (a casual-game experiment, as I recall) were not visible in the summaries. Treat the 2009 findings as qualitative.

## 5. Dynamic difficulty adjustment (DDA): what it says about static curves

### Takeaway
DDA (Hunicke 2005 and later) rests on the premise that **no static curve fits every player**: skill varies too much. Its most-cited early result reduced deaths without players noticing. Reviews describe a large, mostly AI- or heuristic-driven literature with thin empirical testing. Chen and later work argue that *visible or player-chosen* adjustment beats hidden adjustment.

### Cited Findings
- Hunicke, "The case for dynamic difficulty adjustment in games", ACE 2005, pp. 429–433. Hamlet, built on Valve's Half-Life engine, "analyzes and adjust[s] the supply and demand of game inventory in order to control overall game difficulty". It works both reactively (NPC accuracy and damage) and proactively (NPC health before spawning). — [Hunicke's Hamlet paper](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/pubs/Hamlet.pdf); [secondary summary, 2012 paper](https://www.csd.uwo.ca/~mkatchab/pubs/cgames2012.pdf)
- Reported result (**secondhand**, via a 2012 paper): in an experiment with **20 subjects**, DDA cut mean deaths in the first 15 minutes **from 6.4 to 4**, and "the players did not notice the adjustments". — [2012 secondary source](https://www.csd.uwo.ca/~mkatchab/pubs/cgames2012.pdf)
- Zohaib (2018), "Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment (DDA) in Computer Games: A Review", *Advances in Human-Computer Interaction*. I could not reach it. A 2026 paper cites it as one of the reviews that highlighted the variety of adaptive systems proposed and "the need for more empirical testing across genres". — [secondhand via Gold & Dhou 2026 summary](https://www.universityxp.com/research/2026/5/11/rethinking-dynamic-difficulty-adjustment-for-video-game-design)
- A 2025 systematic literature review of DDA (34 studies retained from 547) found approaches mainly use AI techniques (50%) and heuristic or parameter manipulation (47%). — [UFG repository](https://repositorio.bc.ufg.br/items/36654b2c-0c40-4a08-8d21-8ff7f53765c4)
- Stephenson & Renz, "Agent-Based Adaptive Level Generation for Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment in Angry Birds": DDA by generating levels, rather than tuning parameters, in a physics puzzle game. — [arXiv 1902.02518 (preprint)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.02518)
- Sarkar & Cooper (AIIDE 2017 and follow-ups) use rating systems to *order* levels per player ("matchmaking"). Per search summaries, an FDG 2017 online experiment (**n=294**) found that both matchmaking-based ordering and plain difficulty-based ordering produced significantly more attempted and completed levels than random ordering. — [AIIDE 2017 page](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12948); [NSF PAR record (exact mapping to the FDG paper unverified)](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10133941)
- Kristensen, Guckelsberger, Burelli & Hämäläinen, "Personalized Game Difficulty Prediction Using Factorization Machines", UIST 2022. It predicts **how many attempts a specific player will need** on future levels from their attempt counts on earlier levels plus other players' data, using a commercial puzzle game. This is collaborative filtering applied to difficulty. — [arXiv 2209.13495 (preprint)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.13495); [Aalto portal](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/personalized-game-difficulty-prediction-using-factorization-machi/)

### Inferences
- What DDA implies for a *static* campaign: the static order should be set for a target population and percentile, and the variance between players handled by escape valves. In this project those are the skip and the star or ink challenge. Chen's critique of hidden DDA and Juul's responsibility finding both argue against silently easing a level after failures. The project's visible, opt-in skip fits that literature.
- Sarkar & Cooper's result, that difficulty-ordered levels beat random order, is direct evidence that *ordering by measured difficulty* (the 2026-10-12 re-order) is worth doing even without per-player adaptation.

### Gaps
- The Zohaib 2018 review itself, and Hunicke's primary numbers, could not be verified. The 6.4 to 4 figure is secondhand.
- I found no peer-reviewed comparison of a static sawtooth curve against DDA in a puzzle game.

## 6. Predicting human difficulty before release with simulated players, and calibrating against real data

### Takeaway
The industrial and academic pattern, from King, Rovio with Aalto, and Tactile with ITU, is a two-step pipeline. **(1)** Run agents on each level to extract features, chiefly the agent's success rate. **(2)** Fit a regression from those features to *observed* human pass rate or attempts-per-level on already-released levels, then apply it to new ones. Key findings: human-like agents (trained on player moves) correlate better with human difficulty than generic MCTS. **An agent's best-case performance correlates better with human data than its average.** Adding a simple population model (skill, persistence, boredom) improves pass-rate and churn predictions. Combining past-player statistics with agent data beats either alone. For continuous dexterity games, Isaksen et al. predict difficulty with Monte-Carlo simulation of a **motor-noise player model** plus survival analysis.

### Cited Findings
**King: Candy Crush**
- Gudmundsson, Eisen, Poromaa, Nodet, Purmonen, Kozakowski, Meurling & Cao (King / Activision Blizzard), "Human-Like Playtesting with Deep Learning", IEEE CIG 2018, pp. 1–8. A CNN trained on player data from Candy Crush Saga and Candy Crush Soda Saga predicts the move a human would most likely make, and is then used to estimate **level difficulty measured as human success rate**. Compared with MCTS, it "increases correlation with average level difficulty, giving more accurate predictions as well as requiring only a fraction of the computation time". — [ACM DL record](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/CIG.2018.8490442); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Human-Like-Playtesting-with-Deep-Learning-Gudmundsson-Eisen/af7792c1379cc1cb38947d6918d0d232f387c860)
- A secondary write-up says the paper "is a bit vague on the actual numbers" but shows a positive correlation between human and agent success rates. It reports that estimating a new 15-level episode's difficulty fell from **7 days of human playtesting to under a minute** (a claim from the authors' side). — [koaning.io blog (secondary)](https://koaning.io/posts/playtesting)

**Rovio with Aalto: Angry Birds Dream Blast (a physics-based match-3 game)**
- Roohi, Relas, Takatalo, Heiskanen & Hämäläinen, "Predicting Game Difficulty and Churn Without Players", CHI PLAY 2020 (DOI 10.1145/3410404.3414235). DRL (PPO) agents play each level. Ground truth is **168 levels and 95,266 players**; churn is "not playing for 7 days". The baseline maps agent features to pass and churn rates with **simple linear regression**. The contribution adds a population simulation: the baseline pass-rate predictions are **normalised over levels into a difficulty estimate**, and each simulated player has *skill, persistence and boredom*; a simulated player passes when their skill beats the level difficulty. "Player behavior predictions produced by DRL gameplay can be significantly improved by even a very simple population-level simulation of individual player differences." — [arXiv 2008.12937 (preprint)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12937); [Aalto portal](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/predicting-game-difficulty-and-churn-without-players/); [DeepAI summary](https://deepai.org/publication/predicting-game-difficulty-and-churn-without-players)
- Roohi, Guckelsberger, Relas, Heiskanen, Takatalo & Hämäläinen, "Predicting Game Difficulty and Engagement Using AI Players", *PACM HCI* 5 (CHI PLAY 2021), DOI 10.1145/3474658. It adds MCTS, and DRL-enhanced MCTS, to the DRL agents. It also changes feature selection after observing that **"an AI agent's best-case performance can yield stronger correlations with human data than the agent's average performance."** Both changes "consistently improve the prediction accuracy", especially for pass rates. DRL-enhanced MCTS beats both DRL and vanilla MCTS on the **hardest levels**. Same 168-level dataset. The design compares **2 × 2 × 3 = 12 approaches**: two predictive models (the 2020 baseline regression and the population simulation), times DRL or MCTS agents, times three feature-selection strategies. A figure plots the **Spearman correlation between agent features and human pass rates as a function of the percentage of best runs kept**. That is how "best-case" is operationalised: features are computed over the top X% of agent runs rather than all of them. — [arXiv 2107.12061 (preprint)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12061v1); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3474658); [Semantic Scholar figure page](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Predicting-Game-Difficulty-and-Engagement-Using-AI-Roohi-Guckelsberger/fae8e7d0aed5843143488d2df872d9b6f98fbd38/figure/4)

**Tactile with ITU: Lily's Garden (match-3)**
- Kristensen, Valdivia & Burelli, "Statistical Modelling of Level Difficulty in Puzzle Games" (2021). It goes beyond completion rate by modelling the **distribution of moves** in a level parametrically with a **negative binomial**. That fit was good for **~85% of ~4,000 levels**, and better once attempts that used boosters were excluded. The model can estimate how changing the move limit changes completion probability. — [arXiv 2107.03305 (preprint)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03305v1); [Kristensen PhD thesis, ITU 2022](https://pure.itu.dk/files/109068185/PhD_Thesis_Final_version_Jeppe_Kristensen.pdf)
- Kristensen & Burelli, "Difficulty Modelling in Mobile Puzzle Games: An Empirical Study on Different Methods to Combine Player Analytics and Simulated Data", *Int. J. Computer Games Technology* 2024. It operationalises difficulty as **attempts per level**, predicted by supervised regression in a two-step approach "similar to that used by Gudmundsson et al.". The feature groups are historical averages, player features, level attributes and agent-extracted data. The players kept had started in the study window and played at least 200 levels. Difficulty "stabilises at around **3.2 attempts per complete**" (the preprint says "around 3"): easy levels take one attempt, hard ones average over 7, and some need **30 or more**. The attempts distribution is "similar (but not equal) to a **geometric distribution**". **Combining cohort statistics with simulated data gave the most accurate estimates**, and neural networks were the most consistent. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2024/5592373); [arXiv 2401.17436 (preprint)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.17436)
- Same 2024 paper cites Denisova et al. for the link between perceived difficulty and the count of successes and failures. — [search summary of arXiv 2401.17436](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.17436)

**Continuous and dexterity games: simulated motor noise**
- Isaksen, Gopstein & Nealen, "Exploring Game Space Using Survival Analysis", FDG 2015. A parameterised Flappy Bird's difficulty per variant is predicted with "automatic play testing, Monte-Carlo simulation, a **player model based on human motor skills** (precision, reaction time, actions per second), and **exponential survival analysis of score histograms**". The authors argue that much of the difficulty of simple action games comes from motor skill, specifically precision and reaction time. — [author PDF](http://www.nealen.net/papers/exploring-game-space-FDG2015.pdf); [UBC talk abstract](https://cs.ubc.ca/event/2015/10/exploring-game-space-using-survival-analysis-talk-aaron-isaksen-1)
- Isaksen, Gopstein, Togelius & Nealen, "Exploring Game Space of Minimal Action Games via Parameter Tuning and Survival Analysis", *IEEE Trans. Games* 10(2):182–194, 2018. It extends the method to a family of minimal action games and requires a dexterity game in which score relates to lifetime. — [author PDF](http://www.nealen.net/papers/08030128.pdf)
- Isaksen & Nealen, "Comparing Player Skill, Game Variants, and Learning Rates Using Survival Analysis" (AIIDE workshop paper). "When a designer talks about the 'difficulty curve' for a game, they are talking about a **hazard function**." Skill shifts the hazard's intercept. Constant skill and speed give exponential score distributions (constant hazard), while difficulty rising over time gives roughly linear hazards. Learning effects are modelled too. — [AAAI page](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12846); [NYU PDF](https://game.engineering.nyu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/comparing-player-skill-game-variants-and-learning-rates-using-survival-analysis-FINAL.pdf)

**Agent diversity and difficulty**
- In Angry Birds, agent performance is **non-transitive** and varies sharply across levels, which motivated a hyper-agent that picks a sub-agent per level (AIIDE 2017). Stephenson & Renz also note that levels most agents all solve, or all fail, carry little information. — [AIIDE hyper-agent page](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12946); [arXiv 1902.02518 (preprint)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.02518)

### Inferences
- The calibration recipe the literature converges on, for when this game has real players: for each released level, regress observed human **attempts-per-pass** (or log of it) on the simulated features. Those features are breadth, hand rate, the precision floor, the best-case winners' robustness, stroke length and tension. Then use the fitted model to place new levels. This project's TRIES = 1/(breadth × hand rate) is currently an uncalibrated single feature. Roohi's and Kristensen's work says to expect a monotone but *non-identity* relation, and to fit it rather than assume it.
- **"Best-case agent runs correlate better than average runs"** (Roohi 2021) bears directly on this project. Breadth is an *average over plausible strokes*, which corresponds to a random agent. Real players are not random: they draw where the problem looks to be. So a feature like "robustness of the most natural or best winner" (cf. the project's precision floor, which already uses the most forgiving winner) may predict humans better than breadth. That is testable once attempt data exists.
- Isaksen's motor-noise player model is the academic analogue of `tools/test/lib/hand.js`. The literature treats such a model as the *right* tool for continuous-action difficulty, and treats motor precision as a primary source of difficulty, which supports keeping the hand rate as a first-class factor.
- Gudmundsson's point, that human-like agents beat search agents at predicting human difficulty, matches the project's finding that the solver "draws the few strokes that happened to start on the pan" while a person "draws where the problem looks like it is" (SINK). Search-based breadth will be least accurate on levels where human strokes concentrate somewhere other than where the sweep samples.
- The 2024 finding that historical cohort averages plus agent data beats agent data alone means early real-player telemetry from the first chapter would make the simulated predictions for later chapters much better.

### Gaps
- Exact accuracy figures (R², MAE, correlations) for Gudmundsson 2018, Roohi 2020 and Roohi 2021 were not visible in any summary. The arXiv PDFs hold them but were blocked.
- The exact functional forms of Roohi 2020's population simulation (how persistence and boredom drive churn) could not be verified beyond the prose above.
- I found nothing on how many released levels with human data are needed to fit the agent-to-human regression reliably. Roohi used 168 levels; Kristensen used thousands.
- Poromaa's KTH thesis on MCTS for Candy Crush difficulty was not found by search. Its existence is only my background recollection.
- Kristensen & Burelli, "Estimating Player Completion Rate in Mobile Puzzle Games Using Reinforcement Learning" (CoG 2020) appeared in results with no content visible: [arXiv 2306.14626](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.14626).

## 7. Statistical models that turn attempt/pass data into per-level difficulty (with equations)

### Takeaway
There are five families, from simplest to richest:
- **(a)** raw pass rate and attempts-per-pass, which follows a near-geometric distribution;
- **(b)** mixed-effects logistic regression (CNAM group);
- **(c)** item response theory, Rasch/1PL and 2PL, with players as "persons" and levels as "items";
- **(d)** online rating systems, Elo and Glicko-2, that treat each attempt as a match between player and level (Math Garden, Lichess puzzles, Sarkar & Cooper's games);
- **(e)** distributional models (negative binomial over moves; survival and hazard models over score or time).

A rough guide to the data needed: about 30 players per item for a pilot; about 50 for item difficulty within ±1 logit at 99% confidence; about 200 for ±0.5 logit. Lichess puzzle ratings reportedly settle after 20–30 attempts.

### Cited Findings and Equations
**(a) Pass rate and attempts per pass**
- The common industry operationalisation is "the average number of attempts players require to complete a level", or its inverse, the pass rate. — [search summary of Kristensen et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03305v1)
- Attempts-per-level is "similar (but not equal) to a geometric distribution"; mean about 3.2, easy levels 1, hard levels 7+, some 30+. — [Kristensen & Burelli 2024, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2024/5592373)
- **[standard form]** With per-attempt pass probability p_j for level j, the number of attempts N to the first pass is geometric: P(N = n) = (1 − p_j)^(n−1) · p_j, with E[N] = 1/p_j. The estimator is p̂_j = passes_j / attempts_j. "Not equal to geometric" in real data reflects that players learn between attempts (p rises) and that players differ in skill (a mixture of p values), which inflates the tail.

**(b) Mixed-effects logistic regression** — described in Q2 above (CNAM group). **[my formalisation]** logit P(fail_ij) = β0 + β·x_j + u_i, u_i ~ N(0, σ²). Adding attempt number or time as a covariate absorbs learning, which is the "player's apprenticeship" the 2011 paper insists on. — [Springer, Constant et al. 2017](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-67684-5_8)

**(c) Item response theory**
- The Rasch model is the one-parameter logistic (1PL) IRT model. It gives the probability that a person of ability θ succeeds on an item of difficulty b, with items "characterized solely by their difficulty"; infit and outfit mean-squares of 0.5–1.5 indicate productive measurement. — [MetricGate Rasch explainer](https://metricgate.com/calculator/rasch-model)
- **[standard form]** Rasch / 1PL: P(X_ij = 1 | θ_i, b_j) = 1 / (1 + exp(−(θ_i − b_j))). The same 1PL form, as a logistic of ability minus difficulty, is used to generate graded-difficulty levels for agent curricula. — [arXiv 2306.13028 (preprint)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.13028)
- **[standard form, not verified from a fetched source]** 2PL: P = 1 / (1 + exp(−a_j(θ_i − b_j))). The discrimination a_j says how sharply a level separates weak from strong players. A low-a level is one where luck or noise dominates skill.
- Applied to games: a Universitat Politècnica de València thesis fitted a Rasch model (RUMM 2030) to performance measures from recorded play. It concluded Rasch can analyse video-game difficulty *if* the game is strongly unidimensional; some parameters were inconclusive. — [RiuNet (UPV)](https://riunet.upv.es/handle/10251/143530?show=full)
- Sample size (Linacre, *Rasch Measurement Transactions*): with a reasonably targeted sample of **50 persons**, there is **99% confidence** the item difficulty estimate is within **±1 logit** of its stable value; **200 persons** gives 99% confidence within **±0.5 logit**; **30 persons** is enough for a pilot "to see what's happening". — [Rasch.org RMT 7:4](https://www.rasch.org/rmt/rmt74m.htm)

**(d) Elo and Glicko-2: online ratings for player and level together**
- Pelánek (2016, *Computers & Education*), "Applications of the Elo rating system in adaptive educational systems": Elo estimates learner skill and **item difficulty** together, and suits adaptive systems. An **uncertainty function** (K that shrinks with the number of attempts, possibly also with time since the last attempt) gives coarse estimates fast and refines them. Items and learners have very different answer counts, so separate uncertainty functions may be useful. — [author PDF](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S036013151630080X)
- Math Garden (Klinkenberg, Straatemeier & van der Maas 2011, *Computers & Education* 57:1813–1824) is an Elo-based model in which an item replaces the second player. After each answer, ability moves up and item difficulty moves down by **K × (observed score − expected score)**, with the expected score from an extended Rasch model; accuracy *and* response time enter the score. Items are sampled to target a mean success probability of about **0.75**. Item difficulties proved robust over time (Straatemeier 2014). — [MDPI "Tracking with (Un)Certainty" summary](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-3200/8/1/10); [UvA record](https://dare.uva.nl/id/de65bb65-d198-4dbc-ac7b-bc25ca23e627); [Klinkenberg profile](https://www.uva.nl/en/profile/k/l/s.klinkenberg/s.klinkenberg.html)
- **[my formalisation of that description]** With E_ij = 1/(1 + exp(−(θ_i − b_j))) and S_ij ∈ {0, 1}: θ_i ← θ_i + K·(S_ij − E_ij), and b_j ← b_j − K·(S_ij − E_ij). Pelánek's variant replaces a constant K with a decreasing function of the attempt count (exact function not verified).
- Limitations: standard Elo gives **no standard errors** and can inflate rating variance; a 2025 EDM study reports Elo ratings are generally not unbiased, with context-dependent variance. — [MDPI J. Intelligence 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence8010010); [EDM 2025](https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2025/proceedings/2025.EDM.long-papers.99/index.html)
- Sarkar & Cooper, "Level Difficulty and Player Skill Prediction in Human Computation Games", AIIDE 2017. They use **Glicko-2** for both level difficulty and player skill in the puzzle game Paradox: a completed level counts as a *player win* and a failed level as a *level win*, so the levels most often failed get the highest ratings. Regression from player-behaviour and level features predicted eventual ratings better than default or average baselines. Follow-ups predict *scores*: Glicko-2 compares favourably with alternatives except with narrow score ranges and little prior player information (ICEC 2017). "Rating arrays", one rating per score threshold, estimate a full score distribution (FDG 2019). Glicko-2 replay of player-vs-level match data was also used to *infer the difficulty curves* of four games. — [AIIDE 2017](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12948); [ICEC 2017 (IFIP)](https://dl.ifip.org/hal-01771305v1)
- Lichess puzzles: each attempt is treated as a Glicko-2 game between solver and puzzle. Puzzles start from an estimate and move with success and failure; a puzzle's rating reportedly **stabilises after ~20–30 attempts**; themed puzzles give fewer points because the theme is a hint. Forum users note the fit is imperfect, since solving conditions are not a symmetric game. — [Lichess puzzle docs (third-party mirror of lila)](https://mintlify.com/lichess-org/lila/features/puzzles); [Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/puzzle-rating-question) (non-peer-reviewed; lower confidence)
- Predicting a new puzzle's rating *before* anyone plays it is an open competition task. The IEEE BigData Cup 2024 and FedCSIS 2025 challenges ask for Lichess puzzle Glicko-2 ratings from the board and solution alone (~4.56M training puzzles; 2,235 test puzzles in 2025). — [FedCSIS 2025 paper](https://annals-csis.org/Volume_43/drp/pdf/4532.pdf); [GlickFormer summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2410.11078)

**(e) Distributional models**
- **Negative binomial** over moves used within a level (Kristensen et al. 2021): a good fit in ~85% of ~4,000 levels. It lets a designer predict how changing the move budget changes completion probability. — [arXiv 2107.03305 (preprint)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03305v1)
- **Survival and hazard** (Isaksen & Nealen): h(x) = probability of failing at score or time x given survival to x. **[standard form]** survival S(x) = exp(−∫₀ˣ h(u) du), so a constant hazard λ gives S(x) = exp(−λx), the exponential score distribution they report for constant-difficulty play. — [AAAI page](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12846)
- Survival analysis over **playtime and retention** (Allart, Levieux et al.) is covered in Q9.

### Inferences
- For this game the ink budget is to a stroke what the move limit is to a match-3 attempt. Kristensen's negative-binomial idea, a distribution over the resource used rather than pass/fail alone, has a direct analogue: the distribution of *winning stroke lengths*. The project already uses its 20th percentile for three-star and daily budgets. That is a non-parametric version of the same idea.
- With real players, the most practical first model is probably **Elo or Glicko-2 over (player, level) attempts**, as in Sarkar & Cooper and Lichess. It works online, needs no batch refit, handles players of different skill meeting levels in a fixed order (which raw pass rates confound, since later levels are played only by survivors), and per Lichess settles in tens of attempts. A **Rasch/2PL fit** is the batch analogue once a few hundred players per level exist (Linacre: 50 for ±1 logit, 200 for ±0.5 logit). The 2PL discrimination would flag levels where outcomes are noise-dominated, for example a stroke that wins in the solver and loses in the browser.
- **Survivor bias** is the key reason not to use raw pass rate on a linear campaign: only players who passed level k−1 attempt level k, so later levels are measured on a stronger population. IRT, Elo and Glicko, and mixed models with player effects, all correct for this. Raw attempts-per-pass does not.
- Retries violate IRT's assumption of independent responses, since players learn between attempts. Options: count only the *first* attempt per player (pure IRT), model attempt number as a covariate (mixed model), or model attempts-to-first-pass as the outcome (geometric or discrete survival).

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed paper applying Rasch or 2PL IRT specifically to *attempts-to-success on puzzle-game levels* at scale. Sarkar & Cooper's Glicko-2 work is the closest.
- I could not verify Glicko-2's update equations or Pelánek's exact uncertainty function from a fetched source, so they are not reproduced.
- Lichess's "20–30 attempts to stabilise" comes from a third-party mirror and forum, not a paper.
- Linacre's 1994 original note (RMT 7:4 p.328) was not retrieved; the figures above come from the later RMT page.

## 8. Physics-puzzle level generation and playability: Cut the Rope, Angry Birds, EDPCG

### Takeaway
In continuous physics games exhaustive search is impossible, so the literature makes playability tractable in one of three ways: **(1)** prune the action space with a reasoning agent that proposes only "sensible" moves (Shaker et al., Cut the Rope); **(2)** use a portfolio of heterogeneous agents and read difficulty from how many solve the level (Stephenson & Renz, Angry Birds); **(3)** fit survival or hazard curves to Monte-Carlo runs of a noisy player model (Isaksen, Flappy Bird). Experience-driven PCG (Yannakakis & Togelius 2011) frames all of this as a loop: generate, evaluate against a model of player experience, search.

### Cited Findings
- Shaker, Shaker & Togelius, "Evolving Playable Content for Cut the Rope through a Simulation-Based Approach", AIIDE 2013, pp. 72–78, DOI 10.1609/aiide.v9i1.12690. Simulation-based playability checking needs an agent, and building one is hard for Cut the Rope because **continuous time and state make exhaustive search and reactive agents inefficient**. Their answer is a **deliberative Prolog-based agent that proposes the sensible moves at each state**, which "restrict[s] the search space so that depth-first search for solutions become[s] viable". It checks playability inside Ropossum, their grammatical-evolution generator. — [AAAI page](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12690); [ITU record](https://pure.itu.dk/en/publications/evolving-playable-content-for-cut-the-rope-through-a-simulation-b/)
- Ropossum, the authoring tool: a physics engine for simulation, an evolutionary framework, and an AI reasoning agent for playability checks. The earlier Ropossum version did not guarantee solvability. — [ITU record](https://pure.itu.dk/en/publications/ropossum-an-authoring-tool-for-designing-optimizing-and-solving-c/); [Game Developer "Saturday Paper"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-saturday-paper-guaranteed-candy)
- Stephenson & Renz, "Generating varied, stable and solvable levels for Angry Birds style physics games", IEEE CIG 2017. It composes levels from self-contained structures, scored by a fitness function for robustness and variety, and adds **difficulty estimation using agent performance** plus stability and solvability verification. — [ANU portal](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/generating-varied-stable-and-solvable-levels-for-angry-birds-styl/); [Flinders portal](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/generating-varied-stable-and-solvable-levels-for-angry-birds-styl/)
- Stephenson & Renz (DDA via adaptive level generation): levels that most agents either all solve or all fail carry little information. The method is evaluated with several agents using different strategies and AI techniques. — [arXiv 1902.02518 (preprint)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.02518)
- Angry Birds agents are **non-transitive** across levels, which motivated a per-level hyper-agent. — [AIIDE 2017](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12946)
- The IEEE CoG Angry Birds Level Generation Competition requires levels to be stable under gravity and "fun… and challenging to solve", and recommends checking solvability and difficulty with open-source agents. — [CoG 2019 competitions page](https://ewh.ieee.org/conf/cog/2019/competitions_conference)
- Yannakakis & Togelius, "Experience-Driven Procedural Content Generation", *IEEE Trans. Affective Computing* 2(3):147–161, 2011. A taxonomy of PCG plus a framework driven by computational models of player experience: assess content quality as it relates to experience, search the content space, and generate content that best serves the target experience. — [ITU record](https://pure.itu.dk/en/publications/experience-driven-procedural-content-generation/); [2015 extended abstract](https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar//bitstream/123456789/29714/1/Experience-driven_procedural_content_generation_%28extended_abstract%29_2015.pdf)
- Isaksen et al. (Q6): Monte-Carlo runs with a motor-noise player model plus exponential survival analysis predict the difficulty of each variant of a continuous action game. — [author PDF](http://www.nealen.net/papers/exploring-game-space-FDG2015.pdf)

### Inferences
- This project's solver (a sweep over families of plausible strokes, then hand-model robustness) combines Shaker's approach (**restrict to sensible actions**, here the stroke families) with Isaksen's (**Monte-Carlo with a noisy human model**). The literature has no single method better suited to a one-stroke continuous physics puzzle. Its known weakness is the same as here: the "sensible move" generator defines what the agent can find, so levels whose answer falls outside the families read as unsolvable or narrow (cf. the project's "solver aims at top corners" finding).
- Stephenson & Renz's "levels all agents solve or all fail are uninformative", together with non-transitivity, suggests a cheap robustness check: run several differently biased stroke samplers (span, drop, ramp families separately). Treat a level's difficulty as trustworthy only when they agree on its rank. Disagreement is a flag that difficulty depends on the sampler's bias, the Gudmundsson/SINK problem.
- None of these physics-puzzle papers calibrates its agent difficulty against *human* pass rates. They estimate solvability and agent difficulty only. The calibration evidence (Q6) all comes from match-3 games: Candy Crush, Dream Blast, Lily's Garden. Dream Blast is physics-based but not a draw-a-line game.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed work on difficulty estimation for *draw-a-line* physics puzzles (Brain It On, Happy Glass, Draw Climber genre).
- Shaker et al.'s papers include playability but, from the summaries, not a human-calibrated difficulty measure. Unverified, since full text was unavailable.

## 9. Sawtooth / spike-and-relief pacing, and churn caused by difficulty spikes

### Takeaway
Direct experimental evidence for *peaked or oscillating* curves exists but is small: Baumann et al. 2016 (dynamic pacing beats constant balance), ICEC 2022 (curves with peaks motivate most, n=67), and Sarkar & Cooper 2019 (curve transformations change engagement, n=400). Telemetry studies (Allart et al. 2017; Roohi et al. 2020) link difficulty to retention through survival or churn modelling. "Sawtooth" as a named shape is overwhelmingly practitioner lore. **No peer-reviewed study was found showing a sawtooth specifically beats a smooth ramp.**

### Cited Findings
- "Difficulty Pacing Impact on Player Motivation", ICEC 2022 (LNCS 13477, pp. 140–153; CNAM group). Four difficulty curves (two flat, two with different baseline and **peak** levels) tested on **67 video-game-school students** in an FPS: "curves with peaks have the strongest impact on players' motivation." It notes that few authors have studied the *shape* of the difficulty curve rather than DDA. — [HAL record](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03847459)
- Baumann, Lürig & Engeser 2016 (Q1): demand rising and falling with short breaks beat constant balance for flow, and slight overload maximised enjoyment. — [author PDF](https://www.uni-trier.de/fileadmin/fb1/prof/PSY/PGA/unterlagen/BaumannL%C3%BCrigEngeser_Flow_MOEM_2016.pdf)
- Sarkar & Cooper, "Transforming Game Difficulty Curves using Function Composition", CHI 2019 (DOI 10.1145/3290605.3300781). They modified the difficulty curve of the puzzle game Paradox for **400 players**. This affected engagement (levels completed, estimated skill needed to complete them) and perceived competence. Some transformed curves **dominated** others (Pareto-efficient trade-offs among engagement measures). — [NSF PAR PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10133937)
- Allart, Levieux, Pierfitte, Guilloux & Natkin, "Difficulty Influence on Motivation over Time in Video Games using Survival Analysis", FDG 2017. Using Ubisoft telemetry from **Rayman Legends and Tom Clancy's The Division**, they estimate difficulty over time and relate it to retention; the results support **flow and self-efficacy theory**. Early-play differences are explained by attribution (own skill vs avatar strength). — [CNAM record](https://cedric.cnam.fr/index2.php/publis/article/ALP17)
- Same group, "Design Influence on Player Retention" (2016): **time-varying survival analysis on Far Cry 4** data to find periods when players are more likely to quit. — [CNAM record](https://cedric.cnam.fr/index2.php/publis/article/ALN16); [HAL](https://hal-ensta-paris.archives-ouvertes.fr/UPMC/hal-02436692v1)
- Roohi et al. 2020 model per-level **churn** (7 days without play) across 168 Dream Blast levels with agent-predicted difficulty plus simulated persistence and boredom; churn and pass rate are predicted jointly. — [arXiv 2008.12937 (preprint)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12937)
- KDD 2021, "A Difficulty-Aware Framework for Churn Prediction and Intervention in Games", builds difficulty explicitly into churn prediction (title and venue only; content not visible). — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3447548.3467277)
- Sarkar & Cooper report that ordering levels by difficulty or by matchmaking beat random order on levels attempted and completed (n=294). — [AIIDE 2017 page and related work summary](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12948)
- Practitioner description (not peer-reviewed): a sawtooth is peaks each slightly higher than the last, with valleys giving regular relief. Practitioners also warn that "invisible spikes", which only playtest data reveals, are a common failure. — [Bugnet blog](https://bugnet.io/blog/difficulty-curves-how-to-tune-challenge-over-time) (practitioner; low evidential weight)

### Inferences
- The academic support for this project's 10-slot sawtooth (INTRO, three DEVELOPs, SPIKE, BREATHER, two DEVELOPs, BREATHER, BOSS) is **indirect but consistent**. Oscillating or peaked demand beats flat (Baumann; ICEC 2022), ordering by difficulty beats random (Sarkar & Cooper), and the attribution and self-efficacy results say early chapters should build the player's sense that failures are their own and fixable. The specific parameters (ratio 1.26 per chapter, ×4 spike factor, two hard levels in ten) are **not** supported by any paper I found. They are a fit to this game's own simulated data, and CLAUDE.md is right that they need refitting on real attempt counts.
- The churn literature suggests what to measure once live: per-level **quit hazard**, the probability a player who reached level k never returns, using survival analysis as in Allart et al. A spike shows up as a local jump in hazard rather than as a high attempts-per-pass. Since the Lomas and Icy Tower results suggest involvement collapses once a task feels impossible, the hazard at SPIKE and BOSS slots is the number that would validate or refute the ×4 spike factor.
- Constant et al.'s "players underestimate high difficulty" and Lomas's "random-assigned easier keeps players longer" together argue for erring on the easy side of each slot's ×1.5 band for SPIKE and BOSS slots until real data exists.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed sawtooth-vs-ramp comparison found, and no paper deriving an optimal spike frequency (e.g. "every 5th level").
- Effect sizes and the exact curves in the ICEC 2022 (n=67) and Sarkar & Cooper 2019 (n=400) studies were not visible.
- No mobile-telemetry paper was found that isolates a *single* difficulty spike's effect on churn (a natural experiment or A/B test on one level). Industry likely has this data, but I found none published.
- Denisova, Cairns, Guckelsberger & Zendle's **CORGIS** perceived-challenge scale (*IJHCS* 2020) is the validated instrument for measuring *perceived* challenge in playtests. Its sub-dimensions could not be confirmed from summaries. — [York eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/154425/); [CHI EA 2017 precursor](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/21744)
