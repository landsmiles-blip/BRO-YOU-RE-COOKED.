# BRO, YOU'RE COOKED.
## Game Design Bible — Core Concept v0.2

**Working title:** BRO, YOU'RE COOKED.

**Former working title:** SAVE THE GUY

**Genre:** Physics Rescue Puzzle / Physics Sandbox Puzzle

**Platform priority:** YouTube Playables

**Secondary validation platforms:** CrazyGames / Poki / other compatible HTML5 portals where appropriate

**Target format:** Lightweight HTML5 / Canvas / WebGL

**Primary input:** One continuous touch/mouse drawing stroke

**Audience:** General audience, designed for fast comprehension and broad casual appeal; not specifically targeted at children

**Design status:** Core concept / pre-production design bible

**Version:** 0.2

---

# 0. DOCUMENT PURPOSE

This document is the source of truth for the current concept of **BRO, YOU'RE COOKED.** It turns the original “Save the Guy” idea into a concrete, testable game system.

The purpose is not to claim that the game is already proven. It is to define the product precisely enough that a prototype can prove or kill the core idea quickly.

This document distinguishes:

- **LOCKED:** a decision the current design is built around.
- **DESIGN INTENT:** a deliberate direction that may be adjusted after testing.
- **HYPOTHESIS:** something we believe may work but must be validated.
- **OPEN QUESTION:** something intentionally unresolved.
- **DO NOT BUILD YET:** a tempting feature that should not consume prototype budget before the core loop proves itself.

The guiding principle is simple:

> **Do not add complexity to make the game look bigger. Add depth only when it creates better decisions.**

# 1. EXECUTIVE PRODUCT DEFINITION

## 1.1 The game in one sentence

> **Milo is constantly walking into ridiculous danger; the player gets one continuous drawing to create a physical solution that keeps him alive and gets him to safety.**

## 1.2 The emotional promise

The player should feel four things repeatedly:

1. **Alarm:** “Bro, you're cooked.”
2. **Agency:** “I know what I can try.”
3. **Tension:** “Please work… please work…”
4. **Relief/comedy:** “HAHA. That actually worked.”

Then:

> **Again.**

## 1.3 The fundamental interaction

The player does not directly control Milo during the rescue simulation.

The player controls the **environment** by drawing one physical structure.

That distinction is critical.

We are not making another conventional platformer with a drawing gimmick.

We are making a **cause-and-effect puzzle where the player's drawing changes the physics of the world.**

## 1.4 The primary design target

The first 10 seconds must communicate:

- who Milo is;
- where he needs to go;
- what can kill him;
- that the player can draw;
- that the drawing becomes part of the world.

The player should be interacting almost immediately.

The supplied market research identifies startup friction as a major web-game problem, including large abandonment from slow loading and a strong relationship between fast comprehension and play conversion. fileciteturn3file0L383-L408

## 1.5 The strategic target

The prototype is not being judged by how many levels it contains.

It is being judged by whether the mechanic creates this sequence:

> **See danger → draw → watch → fail/succeed → understand → immediately try differently.**

If that loop is not compelling with crude prototype art, polished art will not save it.

# 2. THE MARKET PROBLEM WE ARE SOLVING

YouTube Playables changes the design context compared with traditional game stores and traditional web portals.

The attached research frames Playables as lightweight, instantly accessible experiences embedded in a broader media ecosystem. It also highlights multi-tasking player behavior and the importance of minimizing startup friction. fileciteturn3file0L16-L48

That gives us an unusual product requirement:

## The game must be understandable while the player is distracted.

This does **not** mean the game should be intellectually shallow.

It means the game should postpone complexity until the player has already invested attention.

### Bad complexity

- inventory screens before play;
- tutorials before interaction;
- five currencies;
- character selection;
- long dialogue;
- complicated control schemes.

### Good complexity

- one line can behave differently depending on geometry;
- the same hazard can create a new puzzle when combined with another hazard;
- the player can discover a more elegant solution;
- later levels introduce timing and physical relationships;
- a familiar mechanic is recombined in unfamiliar ways.

The research supports the broader direction: Action/Physics, Arcade/.io, Casual Sports/Skill, Simulation/Tycoon and Spatial/Merge Puzzles are the strongest referenced categories, and successful titles are repeatedly characterized by simple inputs and immediate feedback. fileciteturn3file0L162-L276

But we should not copy those titles.

We should steal the **behavioral principles underneath them**.

# 3. COMPETITIVE AND COMPARABLE GAME ANALYSIS

The most important comparable titles are not useful because we should imitate their art. They are useful because they demonstrate what players already understand.

## 3.1 Save-the-Doge / Save-the-Dog family

Comparable games use a single continuous drawing to create a protective barrier, often against bees and environmental hazards. They commonly allow multiple solutions and encourage shorter/more efficient drawings. citeturn428577search0turn428577search1turn428577search11

### What we should borrow

- single-stroke interaction;
- instant draw-to-physics relationship;
- multiple possible solutions;
- highly readable hazards;
- fast retry;
- simple win condition;
- efficient drawing as optional mastery.

### What we must NOT copy

- static animal sitting in one place;
- bee-defense as the central fantasy;
- identical protective-dome structure;
- meme-animal reskin strategy;
- “survive ten seconds” as the whole game loop.

This is our first major differentiation requirement.

**Milo must move.**

The rescue should be a **sequence**, not a static defense.

---

## 3.2 Love Balls

Love Balls is valuable as a reference because player drawings become physical objects, gravity matters, multiple solutions are encouraged, and drawing efficiency contributes to mastery. citeturn428577search2

### Borrow

- physical drawing objects;
- creative geometry;
- gravity as a system;
- multiple valid solutions;
- ink/drawing efficiency.

### Improve

Our player should not simply manipulate two stationary objects.

Our character should be **continuously moving toward the goal**, which adds urgency and temporal decision-making.

---

## 3.3 Happy Glass

Happy Glass demonstrates the appeal of drawing lines that influence the movement of another system and the value of efficient solutions. Its strongest design lesson is that a short, clear action can produce a surprisingly complex physical result. citeturn428577search7turn428577search10

### Borrow

- immediate physical cause/effect;
- simple objectives;
- efficient drawing as mastery;
- level-by-level difficulty expansion.

### Improve

Our physics should be more dynamic and less dependent on a single liquid-routing fantasy.

---

## 3.4 Draw Climber

Draw Climber shows another valuable principle: the drawing itself can become a functional piece of gameplay geometry, and repeated redraws can create a continuous test-and-adapt loop. citeturn428577search3turn428577search8

### Borrow

- geometry as control;
- adaptation;
- shape affecting physical performance;
- quick rounds;
- simple input with skill depth.

### Improve

We should **not** continuously redraw during the rescue in V1.

The one-drawing constraint creates stronger decision pressure and differentiates our game.

# 4. THE DIFFERENTIATION THESIS

The crowded part of the market is:

> **Draw a wall around an animal.**

Our territory is:

> **Draw one physical intervention into a moving rescue sequence.**

That creates five differentiators.

## 4.1 The character moves

The player is not protecting a static target.

Milo is actively moving toward safety.

## 4.2 The goal is directional

There is a clear destination.

Start → danger → exit.

## 4.3 The drawing is an intervention

The player can use geometry as:

- shield;
- bridge;
- ramp;
- wedge;
- catcher;
- redirector;
- launcher;
- support;
- counterweight;
- trap.

## 4.4 Time matters

The player does not have infinite contemplation.

Milo is moving.

The environment is changing.

## 4.5 The game has personality

The title itself is a reaction:

> **BRO, YOU'RE COOKED.**

The game should feel like the player is constantly bailing out a spectacularly unlucky idiot.

## 5.2 Brand personality

The title should communicate:

- danger;
- comedy;

# 6. PLAYER FANTASY

The player should feel like:

> **the only competent person in Milo's life.**

Milo is not stupid in a frustrating way.

He is **comically unlucky**.

He keeps moving forward because he trusts the player to save him.

That relationship gives the game personality without dialogue trees.

## 6.1 Character behavior

Milo should:

- walk/run automatically;
- react to collisions;
- stumble;
- panic near hazards;
- briefly celebrate after success;
- have exaggerated but readable physical reactions.

## 6.2 No dialogue dependency

V1 should not require voice acting or large text blocks.

His body language does the communication.

# 7. CHARACTER DESIGN — MILO

## 7.1 Visual role

Milo must be immediately readable at a small scale.

Silhouette priorities:

- oversized head;
- compact body;
- obvious face;
- simple limbs;
- distinctive accessory.

The accessory could be a tiny helmet or backpack to create a recognizable silhouette.

## 7.2 Animation requirements

Minimum V1 set:

- idle/ready;
- run;
- stumble;
- fall;
- impact;
- near-miss reaction;
- victory;
- defeat.

## 7.3 Design constraint

Do not make the character visually expensive.

The game should remain attractive primarily through:

- motion;
- physics;
- reactions;
- environmental events.

# 8. CORE GAMEPLAY LOOP

## 8.1 Macro loop

```text
ENTER LEVEL
↓
SEE MILO + HAZARD + GOAL
↓
DRAW ONE CONTINUOUS STROKE
↓
RELEASE
↓
PHYSICS / MOVEMENT PLAYS OUT
↓
MILO SURVIVES OR FAILS
↓
SUCCESS / FAILURE FEEDBACK
↓
NEXT LEVEL OR INSTANT RETRY
```

## 8.2 Micro loop

```text
Predict
↓
Draw
↓
Observe
↓
Evaluate
↓
Adjust mental model
↓
Retry
```

The second loop is where the game becomes a game rather than a novelty.

# 9. THE ONE-DRAWING RULE

## 9.1 Locked rule

Each standard level gives the player **one continuous drawing stroke**.

The player presses, drags, and releases.

Once released, the drawing becomes physical.

No second drawing is permitted during the simulation in the standard mode.

## 9.2 Why this matters

Without the restriction, players can brute-force the level with a pile of lines.

With one stroke:

> **the drawing becomes a decision.**

## 9.3 What counts as one stroke

A stroke begins on pointer/touch down and ends on pointer/touch up.

The game stores the path as sampled points.

The path is simplified and converted into a playable physical body.

## 9.4 Drawing restrictions

Potential restrictions can be introduced per level:

- maximum line length;
- no-draw zones;
- required anchor zones;
- minimum/maximum shape size;
- forbidden overlap with Milo;
- forbidden overlap with goal.

These are **advanced content tools**, not launch complexity.

---

# 10. DRAWING-TO-PHYSICS PIPELINE

The line is not decorative.

It must become an actual game object.

## 10.1 Pipeline

```text
Player Input
→ sampled path
→ smoothing
→ point reduction
→ stroke validation
→ geometry generation
→ collision shape
→ physical body
→ simulation
```

## 10.2 Geometry generation

The player sees a continuous hand-drawn stroke.

Internally, we can convert it to:

- a polyline collider;
- or a sequence of connected segments;
- with carefully controlled thickness.

The prototype should prefer simplicity over an elaborate freeform mesh.

## 10.3 Physical properties

V1 should start with:

- fixed density;
- fixed friction;
- fixed restitution/bounciness;
- fixed thickness.

Do not expose materials to the player initially.

## 10.4 Mass and stability

The line must have believable weight.

However, the physics should be tuned for game readability rather than scientific accuracy.

The player must be able to understand failures.

---

# 11. FAIR PHYSICS PRINCIPLES

This is one of the most important systems in the whole game.

## Rule 1 — Same inputs should create substantially the same outcome.

Physics puzzles need predictable experimentation.

## Rule 2 — Failure must have an obvious cause.

The player should think:

> “My ramp was too shallow.”

not:

> “The engine screwed me.”

## Rule 3 — Avoid invisible forces.

Do not secretly alter physics simply to force the intended solution unless clearly communicated.

## Rule 4 — Use forgiveness where human drawing is imprecise.

The system should tolerate reasonable strokes.

## Rule 5 — Prefer dramatic readable physics over microscopic accuracy.

The game is a puzzle, not a physics simulator.

---

# 12. MILO MOVEMENT MODEL

## 12.1 Automatic movement

Milo normally moves toward the level's goal without direct directional control.

This preserves the central mental model:

> **The player changes the environment.**

## 12.2 Movement speed

Base speed should be tuned to create planning time without destroying tension.

We should expose speed as a level parameter rather than a global hard-coded value.

Example conceptual parameters:

```text
walkSpeed
runSpeed
acceleration
jumpAbility
fallTolerance
stunDuration
recoveryDuration
```

## 12.3 Jumping

Do not give Milo a normal platformer jump button in V1.

He can be launched, bounced, pushed, or carried through physics.

This preserves the “indirect control” identity.

---

# 13. THE GOAL SYSTEM

The goal should be visually obvious.

Recommended motif:

### EXIT / SAFE ZONE

A bright, visually stable destination.

Potential presentation:

- door;
- rescue flag;
- safe platform;
- glowing checkpoint.

The goal must communicate:

> **Milo needs to get there.**

## 13.1 Level completion

The level completes when Milo enters the goal's success trigger while alive and in a valid state.

## 13.2 Timing

If the player survives but does not reach the goal, the level is not automatically complete.

This is the key difference from static protect-the-character games.

---

# 14. FAILURE CONDITIONS

A level can fail when:

- Milo dies from a hazard;
- Milo falls out of the playable space;
- Milo becomes trapped beyond a defined recovery threshold;
- a required objective becomes physically impossible;
- a timed rescue expires in levels where time is explicitly part of the challenge.

## 14.1 No ambiguous failure

Avoid arbitrary “you lose” rules that the player could not anticipate.

---

# 15. THE LEVEL COMPOSITION MODEL

Every level is built from five elements:

### A. START

Where Milo begins.

### B. GOAL

Where Milo must end.

### C. HAZARD

What makes the path unsafe.

### D. ENVIRONMENT

Static geometry that shapes the solution.

### E. DRAWING OPPORTUNITY

The physical intervention the player can create.

The designer then adds optional:

### F. SECONDARY SYSTEM

A switch, moving object, chain reaction, etc.

---

# 16. THE LEVEL READABILITY RULE

A player should be able to identify:

**Milo → danger → goal**

without reading instructions.

The level should ideally communicate itself through visual hierarchy.

Recommended hierarchy:

1. Milo;
2. danger;
3. goal;
4. environmental objects;
5. decorative background.

Decorative elements must never compete with the puzzle.

# 17. LEVEL DESIGN GRAMMAR

This is the content-generation language for the game.

## 17.1 BLOCK

Use the drawing to stop an object.

Example:

rolling boulder → barrier → safe path.

## 17.2 BRIDGE

Span a gap.

Example:

Milo → pit → exit.

## 17.3 RAMP

Redirect movement or launch Milo/object.

## 17.4 CATCH

Catch something falling.

## 17.5 SHIELD

Protect Milo from a moving threat.

## 17.6 REDIRECT

Change the trajectory of a hazard.

## 17.7 WEDGE

Create a jam or mechanical support.

## 17.8 SUPPORT

Hold up an object.

## 17.9 COUNTERWEIGHT

Use mass and leverage.

## 17.10 FUNNEL

Guide a rolling or falling object into a useful position.

## 17.11 TRIGGER

Create a physical chain reaction.

## 17.12 LAUNCH

Use an object/ramp to send Milo toward a safe area.

These are **verb categories**, not level types. A single level can combine several verbs.

# 18. THE BIG DIFFERENTIATOR: RESCUE SEQUENCES

A major design improvement over the original concept is the idea of **multi-event rescue**.

A simple level can have:

```text
Boulder falls
↓
Ramp redirects boulder
↓
Boulder hits switch
↓
Gate opens
↓
Milo crosses bridge
↓
Exit
```

One drawing.

Multiple consequences.

This creates emergent moments without adding more player controls.

That is exactly the kind of depth we want.

---

# 19. LEVEL DIFFICULTY — HOW TO MAKE IT HARD WITHOUT MAKING IT COMPLICATED

The game becomes harder by increasing:

- number of relevant objects;
- timing sensitivity;
- physical relationships;
- consequence chains;
- line-efficiency pressure;
- movement speed;
- spatial constraints.

The game does **not** become harder primarily by adding controls.

## 19.1 Difficulty ladder

### Tier 1 — Obvious solution

One hazard.

One obvious line.

### Tier 2 — Geometry

Same hazard.

Better shape required.

### Tier 3 — Timing

The correct shape must interact with movement.

### Tier 4 — Multi-object

One line affects two objects.

### Tier 5 — Chain reaction

A → B → C → Milo.

### Tier 6 — Choice

Multiple plausible solutions.

### Tier 7 — Mastery

Efficient solution becomes difficult.

---

# 20. THE THREE-LAYER PUZZLE MODEL

Every excellent level should ideally contain three cognitive layers.

## Layer 1 — Immediate problem

> What is going to kill Milo?

## Layer 2 — Physical solution

> What can my line do about it?

## Layer 3 — Optimization

> Can I do it more cleverly?

A level that only has Layer 1 becomes trivial.

A level that requires Layer 3 before the player can even understand Layer 1 becomes frustrating.

---

# 21. MULTIPLE SOLUTIONS

Multiple solutions are encouraged.

This is one of the strongest transferable lessons from comparable physics drawing games. Save-the-Doge-style games explicitly promote multiple answers, while Love Balls treats its physics space as open-ended. citeturn428577search0turn428577search2

But we need a distinction:

> **Multiple solutions ≠ uncontrolled solutions.**

The designer must create a bounded physical system in which several reasonable strategies can succeed.

## 21.1 Intended solution

The clean solution the level designer expects.

## 21.2 Alternate solution

A legitimate physical solution using a different construction.

## 21.3 Emergent solution

An unexpected but valid interaction produced by the system.

The third category is especially valuable.

---

# 22. STARS AND MASTERY

Completion should be easy enough to feel rewarding.

Mastery should be harder.

## 22.1 Proposed rating

### 1 STAR — RESCUED

Milo survives and reaches safety.

### 2 STARS — CLEAN

Player uses reasonably efficient drawing / solution.

### 3 STARS — COOKED NO MORE

Player achieves an excellent efficiency or solves the level under the highest constraint.

The exact thresholds should be data-driven per level.

## 22.2 Why this matters

It creates two different player motivations:

**Completion:**

> “I beat it.”

**Mastery:**

> “I can beat it better.”

|---|---|
| Wood | Standard |

# 27. THE COMEDY SYSTEM

The game should be funny **because of the simulation**, not because of jokes written on screen.

Examples:

### Near miss

Milo walks past a falling hammer by one pixel.

He pauses.

Looks upward.

Keeps walking.

### Bad drawing

The structure collapses.

Milo gets launched into the sky.

Tiny stunned face.

### Ridiculous success

A player accidentally creates a giant ricochet.

Milo flies safely into the exit.

The game should celebrate the absurdity.

# 29. UX / SCREEN ARCHITECTURE

## 29.1 Level screen

Primary play area should dominate the screen.

Minimal elements:

- level number / tiny progress indicator;
- optional stars once earned;
- restart button;
- pause/mute handled appropriately.

## 29.2 No clutter

Avoid:

- currency counters during active puzzle play;
- unnecessary toolbars;
- large permanent menus;
- intrusive banners.

## 29.3 Failure screen

Failure should be nearly instantaneous.

Suggested sequence:

```text
Milo fails
↓
comic reaction
↓
RETRY
```

The player should return to drawing immediately.

---

# 30. RETRY PSYCHOLOGY

Fast retries are central.

The player needs enough feedback to form a new hypothesis but not enough interruption to break the loop.

Ideal emotional rhythm:

> “Oops.”

> “I know.”

> “Again.”

If a failed attempt takes 15 seconds to restart, the game is doing damage to itself.

## 31.1 Primary progression

## 31.2 Secondary progression

## 31.3 Long-term progression

Potential future systems:

- outfits;

- rescue badges;

# 32. SESSION ARCHITECTURE

The research suggests that longer sessions are commercially useful and that puzzle/simulation categories show particularly strong session-depth signals in the referenced portal data. fileciteturn3file0L401-L408

We should therefore build for **depth without forcing long levels**.

## 32.1 Target session structure

### 0–1 minute

Fast wins.

### 1–3 minutes

Mechanic learning.

### 3–5 minutes

First meaningful failures.

### 5–8 minutes

System combinations.

### 8–12+ minutes

Mastery / challenge / “one more.”

This is a design target, not a promise of algorithmic reward.

---

# 33. THE SECOND LOOP: MASTERY

The basic gameplay loop gets players started.

Mastery keeps them around.

```text
Complete level
↓
Notice inefficient solution
↓
Replay
↓
Shorter line
↓
Cleaner chain reaction
↓
Better rating
```

This is particularly important because a simple physics game can otherwise be consumed and discarded after a few minutes.

---

# 34. REWARDED AD ECONOMY

The attached research identifies rewarded ads as the strongest eCPM/retention opportunity among the referenced ad formats and specifically points to extra lives, hints, skips and multipliers as natural exchanges. fileciteturn3file0L425-L464

The monetization design should therefore be built around **player desire**, not interruption.

## 34.1 Primary rewarded option — UNDO DRAW

After releasing the drawing, the player realizes:

> “That was stupid.”

Option:

**WATCH → UNDO DRAW**

The player gets to redraw.

This is directly tied to the core mechanic.

## 34.2 Secondary — HINT

A rewarded hint should reveal a useful relationship, not dump the entire solution.

Examples:

- highlight an anchor;
- pulse a dangerous object;
- show an arrow indicating likely trajectory.

## 34.3 Future — REWIND

After a very late failure:

**WATCH → REWIND**

Return to just before the fatal event.

Do not implement until the base loop proves itself.

---

# 35. INTERSTITIAL STRATEGY

The research recommends delaying interstitials until after early progression because early interruption can damage retention. fileciteturn3file0L425-L442

Therefore:

**No interstitial before meaningful player investment.**

Potential trigger candidates:

- world completion;
- after a block of levels;
- after multiple retries;
- at a natural rest state.

Exact implementation must follow the current YouTube Playables monetization rules at deployment time.

# 36. TECHNICAL PLATFORM CONSTRAINTS

The source research describes Playables as a closed sandbox with no general external network access and a strict initial payload ceiling. It also calls out the `firstFrameReady()`, `gameReady()`, pause/mute handling and native persistence lifecycle. fileciteturn3file0L50-L160

Current official Google documentation should be treated as the final authority during implementation and certification.

The most important engineering implications are:

- keep the initial bundle extremely small;
- prefer a target comfortably below the hard ceiling;
- do not depend on a custom backend;
- do not build network multiplayer into V1;
- support pause/resume/mute lifecycle correctly;
- support touch and mouse input;
- support responsive aspect ratios;
- preserve state through supported platform storage;
- keep assets optimized from the beginning.

---

# 37. ENGINEERING TARGET: BUILD SMALLER THAN NECESSARY

The research records a 30 MB initial-build ceiling. Current Google documentation recommends a substantially smaller starting footprint than that ceiling.

Therefore our internal rule is:

> **15 MB target, 30 MB absolute ceiling.**

We should not build a 29 MB game because the platform technically permits it.

Smaller means:

- faster startup;
- less risk;
- easier iteration;
- more room for future content.

# 42. DETERMINISM REQUIREMENT

Physics-puzzle experimentation becomes frustrating when the same stroke produces radically different results.

The game should therefore use tightly controlled simulation parameters and deterministic behavior wherever practical.

Randomness may be used later for specific challenge modes, but not in the core puzzle logic unless the randomness itself is clearly communicated.

# 50. FIRST 10 LEVELS

The first ten levels are not merely content.

They are the game's onboarding system.

## Level 1 — WALL

Milo walks toward a rolling ball.

Draw a simple barrier.

Purpose:

> Teach that the line blocks things.

## Level 2 — GAP

Milo must cross a pit.

Draw a bridge.

Purpose:

> Teach that the line can become terrain.

## Level 3 — RAMP

Milo needs vertical assistance.

A flat bridge fails.

Angled structure succeeds.

Purpose:

> Teach that shape matters.

## Level 4 — CATCH

An object falls.

Draw a cradle.

Purpose:

> Teach that the line can catch things.

## Level 5 — REDIRECT

A rolling boulder approaches.

Use an angled structure to redirect it.

Purpose:

> Teach trajectory manipulation.

## Level 6 — SUPPORT

A platform collapses unless supported.

Purpose:

> Teach structural thinking.

## Level 7 — MOVE THEN HIT

Milo crosses while a secondary object moves.

Purpose:

> Introduce timing.

## Level 8 — CHAIN REACTION

Drawing changes the path of one object, which activates another.

Purpose:

> Reveal system interactions.

## Level 9 — TWO POSSIBLE SOLUTIONS

Allow two clear solutions.

Purpose:

> Teach creative freedom.

## Level 10 — MASTER LEVEL

Combine wall + ramp + timing.

Purpose:

> Confirm the game can now demand thinking while retaining one input.

# 51. CONTENT CURVE

The first world should feel generous.

Players should succeed frequently enough to understand that they are capable.

Then the game should create controlled failure.

Suggested qualitative curve:

```text
Very Easy
  ↓
Easy
  ↓
Easy + new idea
  ↓
Medium
  ↓
Medium + timing
  ↓
Hard
  ↓
Hard + combinations
  ↓
Mastery
```

Avoid sudden spikes unless the spike is itself the intended challenge.

# 52. CONTENT QUANTITY

Do not start by promising 500 levels.

## MVP

**20–30 levels** enough to validate the loop.

## First content release target

**60 carefully authored levels.**

## Expansion

100+ after retention is proven.

Why not 1,000?

Because weak levels are not an asset.

A level that makes a player quit is negative inventory.

# 53. LEVEL QUALITY CHECKLIST

Every level should answer YES to most of these:

- Can I understand the danger quickly?
- Can I see the goal?
- Do I know what my drawing can affect?
- Is there at least one satisfying idea to discover?
- Is failure understandable?
- Is the physics deterministic enough to learn from?
- Can a clever player find an alternate solution?
- Does replaying have a reason?
- Is the level visually readable in portrait mode?
- Does the level work without a text tutorial?

If not, the level needs work or should be cut.

---

# 54. ANTI-FRUSTRATION SYSTEMS

Potentially useful:

## Ghost preview

Very subtle visualization of where the stroke will land while drawing.

Do not overdo this because it can remove discovery.

## Safe fail

Some failures should produce near misses rather than instant death.

## Generous collision margins

Milo should not die because his toe touched a hazard by one pixel unless precision is explicitly the lesson.

## Rapid retry

Always available.

---

# 55. THE "AHA" TEST

A great level creates a moment like:

> “Oh. I can use that thing.”

or:

> “Wait... if I angle it like that...”

or:

> “I don't have to block it. I can send it somewhere else.”

These are stronger than:

> “I memorized the solution.”

We are designing for **discovery**, not rote execution.

---

# 56. THE PLAYER SHOULD NOT NEED TO KNOW PHYSICS

The game should teach physical principles implicitly.

For example:

Player draws a ramp.

Object rolls.

Player learns angle.

No explanation:

> “This object will accelerate due to gravitational potential energy.”

Absolutely not.

The game teaches through consequence.

---

# 57. WHAT COUNTS AS “TOO EASY”

A level is too easy if:

- the first obvious stroke succeeds every time;
- there is no reason to observe physics;
- the player does not learn anything;
- replay is pointless.

## What counts as the right difficulty

The player should often think:

> “I know what to do. I just need to do it better.”

That is healthy difficulty.

---

# 58. WHAT COUNTS AS “TOO HARD”

A level is too hard if:

- the player cannot infer why they failed;
- the result depends on tiny drawing differences;
- the intended physical behavior is unclear;
- the solution requires hidden information;
- the level is only solvable through trial-and-error repetition with no learning.

# 69. KILL CRITERIA

We are not emotionally attached to the concept.

- players cannot understand the game quickly;
- drawing feels imprecise or frustrating;
- physics outcomes feel random;
- players rarely retry;
- levels become repetitive after a few minutes;
- there is no meaningful mastery layer;
- the game is only fun because of presentation rather than interaction;
- the core mechanic cannot generate new level grammar cheaply;
- the game becomes a clone of existing draw-to-protect products.

A beautiful failure is still a failure.

Prototype the loop before polish.

**Visible problem**

+

**obvious stakes**

+

**surprising solution**

+

**strong physical payoff**

# 84. PLAYER SKILL MODEL

There are three primary skills.

## Skill 1 — Spatial prediction

Where will this shape land?

## Skill 2 — Physical intuition

What will the object do?

## Skill 3 — Efficiency

Can I solve it with less?

These skills deepen naturally without adding buttons.

---

# 85. DESIGN PRINCIPLE: EASY INPUT, HARD JUDGMENT

This is one of the most important statements in the entire Bible.

> **The input must be simple. The decision can be difficult.**

Press and drag is simple.

Deciding what to draw is the game.

That is how we avoid both extremes:

### Too complicated

Controls overwhelm player.

### Too easy

Nothing interesting to decide.

The sweet spot is:

> **one simple action with increasingly difficult consequences.**

---

# 86. DESIGN PRINCIPLE: PLAYER AGENCY WITHOUT CONTROL CLUTTER

Milo's automatic movement is not a limitation.

It is a deliberate constraint.

The player thinks:

> “I can't control him. I have to fix the world.”

That makes every drawing meaningful.

---

# 87. DESIGN PRINCIPLE: FAIL FAST, LEARN FAST

The ideal failed attempt teaches something.

Example:

Attempt 1:

> Wall falls over.

Player learns:

> Need an anchor.

Attempt 2:

> Wall holds but boulder rolls underneath.

Player learns:

> Need lower placement.

Attempt 3:

> Milo survives.

That is a strong learning loop.

---

# 88. DESIGN PRINCIPLE: DON'T HIDE THE PUZZLE INSIDE THE UI

The puzzle should exist in the physical world.

Not in:

- menus;
- text;
- inventory screens;
- dialogue;
- tooltips.

The player should be solving the world itself.

---

# 89. DESIGN PRINCIPLE: VISIBLE CAUSALITY

When something happens, the game should make it clear why.

Bad:

Milo dies.

No obvious cause.

Good:

Boulder hits barrier.

Barrier tips.

Boulder rolls into Milo.

Player sees the chain.

That chain is the lesson.

---

# 90. DESIGN PRINCIPLE: THE GAME SHOULD SURPRISE WITHOUT CHEATING

Unexpected outcomes are good.

Unfair outcomes are bad.

The difference:

### Surprise

“I didn't think the ball would bounce there!”

### Cheat

“The game secretly changed gravity.”

We want surprise.

Give a player the game without explanation.

9. Do they laugh?
